Two H Atoms: What Changes When They Bond?

Learning Objective: Recall the wave description and identify the question MO theory answers.

Start with two separate H atoms

H · + · H → H–H

Each H atom: one 1s atomic orbital, one electron.

Wave A and Wave B are drawn on separate baselines. The gold curve shows their sum.

  • Recall Topic 7.3: an orbital is described by a wavefunction, ψ. Its |ψ|² gives electron probability density.
  • Recall interference: wave descriptions can reinforce or cancel when combined.
  • Our question: when two atomic orbitals combine, where can the two electrons go?

Two 1s Orbitals Give Two Possible Combinations

Learning Objective: Connect phase, electron density and energy in one comparison.

1 · Same phase → bonding

More electron density between nuclei; lower energy.

2 · Opposite phase → antibonding

Node between nuclei; higher energy.

Green / violet = opposite orbital phases. These colors do not represent electric charge.

Two atomic orbitals (AOs) → two molecular orbitals (MOs).

Next question: We have two molecular orbitals. Which one receives the electrons?
  • Phase = sign of ψ. Two colors show the two signs; neither color means electric charge.
  • Same phase in the overlap region: reinforce → greater density between nuclei → bonding MO.
  • Opposite phase: cancel at the middle plane → node → antibonding MO.
  • MO = molecular orbital: an orbital extending over the molecule. Both combinations are available; electron occupancy determines net bonding.

H₂: From Energy Levels to One Net Bond

Learning Objective: Fill the available molecular orbitals and interpret their occupancy.

Follow the same gold electron markers from each AO into the MOs. The motion shows electron bookkeeping, not a physical trajectory.

Bond order=bonding e⁻ − antibonding e⁻2
H₂: two electrons in the lower MO → net stabilization → stable H–H bond.
Next question: If electrons also fill the upper orbital, does the molecule gain another bond?
  • Read vertically: higher on the diagram = higher energy.
    Left and right: atomic orbitals (AO).
    Center: molecular orbitals (MO).
  • Names: lower = σ₁s; upper = σ*₁s.
    σ recalls overlap along the bond axis; * means antibonding.
  • Count and fill: 1 + 1 = 2 e⁻ → lower σ₁s, ↑↓.
    Lowest energy first; maximum two e⁻ per orbital.
  • Bond order measures net bonding. A pair in a bonding MO contributes +1 to bond order. A pair in an antibonding MO contributes −1. Count the electrons in each kind of MO and divide their difference by two.
    1 → one net bond (a single bond).
    0 → no net covalent bond in this model.
    For the same pair of elements, higher bond order usually means a shorter, stronger bond.
  • H₂ bond order 2 − 02 = [ _______ ] → one net H–H bond.
    Only the lower MO is occupied → net stabilization.

He₂: What If Both Orbitals Are Filled?

Learning Objective: Use the same model to test the effect of antibonding electrons.

Follow the same gold electron markers from each AO into the MOs. The motion shows electron bookkeeping, not a physical trajectory.

Bond order=bonding e⁻ − antibonding e⁻2
He₂: an antibonding pair offsets the bonding pair → no net covalent bonding.
  • Change the atoms: each He contributes two electrons.
    Total = 2 + 2 = 4 e⁻.
  • First pair: lower than the original AO 1s level → bonding stabilization in σ₁s.
  • Second pair: higher than the original AO 1s level → antibonding occupancy in σ*₁s.
  • He₂ bond order 2 − 22 = [ _______ ].
  • Why He₂ gives zero
    The σ₁s pair favors bonding (+1). The σ*₁s pair occupies an orbital with a node between the nuclei and opposes bonding (−1). With both pairs present, this model predicts no ordinary He–He covalent bond.

Connect Back to σ and π

Learning Objective: Separate orbital symmetry from bonding or antibonding character.

σ · p_z end-on combination

Bond axis z: left → right.

π · pₓ side-on combination

Same bond axis z; density above and below.

Green / violet = opposite orbital phases. These colors do not represent electric charge.

Next question: Use the model to explain an electron arrangement, not simply memorize another diagram.
  • Recall T9.5: end-on overlap gives σ; side-by-side p overlap gives π.
  • What MO theory adds: each suitable pair of AOs produces a bonding and an antibonding combination.
  • Read the labels: σ or π describes symmetry; * identifies the antibonding combination.
  • Keep the earlier bond count: double = 1 σ + 1 π; triple = 1 σ + 2 π.
