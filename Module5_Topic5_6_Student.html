Kinetic Molecular Theory — Start with Moving Gas Particles

Learning Objective: Describe the particle model used in the Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT) of gases.
Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT) explains gas behavior by treating a gas as many tiny particles in constant motion.
↗ ↘ → ← ↙

Moving

rapid, random motion

Far apart

mostly empty space

Tiny particles

particle volume is negligible in the ideal model

Interactions

ideal particles have negligible attractions and repulsions

Collisions

collisions are elastic; kinetic energy is transferred, not lost overall

The key starting point: gas particles are moving.

Temperature, Average KE, and Molecular Speed

Learning Objectives: Connect particle motion, kinetic energy, and Kelvin temperature without treating the relationship as an unexplained assumption. Explain why gases at the same temperature can have the same average translational kinetic energy but different molecular speeds.
Kinetic energy is energy of motion. For one moving particle, KE = ½mv².
average translational KE = 3/2 kBT
For a large gas sample, absolute temperature determines the average translational kinetic energy.
Why average?

individual molecules have different speeds

Why translational?

we mean motion from place to place through the container

Why T?

kB is constant, so average translational KE ∝ T

Where this comes from: when the particle model for gas pressure is connected with the experimentally established ideal-gas relationship, the result is that average translational kinetic energy depends only on absolute temperature.
Same temperature: He and Xe have the same average translational kinetic energy.

He — lighter

smaller mass

Xe — heavier

larger mass

KE = 12mv²
same average KE→ smaller mass→ larger speed

Compare different gases at the same T

lighter gas → faster

Compare the same gas at different T

higher T → larger average KE → faster

Molecular speed depends on both temperature and particle mass.

KMT Explains the Gas Laws — and PV = nRT Connects Them

Learning Objective: Use particle-level reasoning to explain the four simple gas-law trends and connect them to the ideal gas law.

Boyle: V ↓ → P ↑

same particles, less space → more frequent wall collisions

Gay-Lussac: T ↑ → P ↑

greater average translational KE → faster, more forceful wall collisions

Charles: T ↑ → V ↑

faster particles → flexible container expands to maintain pressure

Avogadro: n ↑ → V ↑

more particles → container expands to maintain pressure

Boyle Charles Gay-Lussac Avogadro → PV = nRT
Big picture: the simple gas laws describe separate experimental relationships among P, V, n, and T. The ideal gas law combines them quantitatively, while KMT explains the same behavior at the particle level.
KMT tells us why. PV = nRT tells us how much.

RMS Speed — Meaning + Worked Calculation

Learning Objectives: Explain why one representative molecular-speed value is useful and introduce root-mean-square speed. Apply the KMT-derived RMS-speed relationship by converting temperature and molar mass before substitution.
A gas sample contains molecules moving at many instantaneous speeds. RMS speed gives one useful representative speed for the sample.
urms = √(3RT/M)

Temperature

T ↑ → urms ↑

Molar Mass

M ↑ → urms ↓

Worked problem: Calculate urms for Cl₂(g) at 37.0 °C. M(Cl₂) = 70.90 g/mol.
Path: °C → K; g/mol → kg/mol; then apply urms.
T = 37.0 + 273.15 = 310.15 K
70.90 g/mol × 1 kg1000 g = 0.07090 kg/mol
urms = √3(8.314 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(310.15 K)0.07090 kg/mol
urms ≈ 330 m/s
Reasonableness: hundreds of meters per second is a realistic molecular-speed scale for a gas near room temperature.

Diffusion vs Effusion — Molecular Motion in Action

Learning Objective: Distinguish diffusion from effusion using particle-level representations.
KMT connection: gas particles are constantly moving. Diffusion and effusion are observable consequences of that molecular motion.

Diffusion

Different gases mix throughout the same space.

Effusion

Gas particles pass through a tiny hole into another space.

→

Diffusion

mixing across the available volume

Effusion

escape through a very small opening

Both depend on molecular motion. Graham’s law in this Topic is mainly used for effusion-rate comparisons.

Graham’s Law — Read the Ratio + Worked O₂/Kr Comparison

Learning Objectives: Interpret a single-gas effusion rate, set up a two-gas rate ratio correctly, and explain the meaning of the numerical answer. Calculate and interpret a two-gas effusion-rate ratio.

First: one gas can have an effusion rate

The rate of effusion tells how much gas passes through a tiny opening per unit time.

Example: 12.0 mL/min

Graham’s law becomes useful when we want to compare the effusion rates of two gases.
Read the wording first: “How many times faster does oxygen effuse than krypton?”

Rate fraction

The gas named as the one being compared — oxygen — goes on top.

The reference gas — krypton — goes on the bottom.

Molar-mass fraction

Under the square root, reverse that order:

M(krypton) on top
M(oxygen) on bottom
rate of oxygenrate of krypton = √M(krypton)M(oxygen)

If the answer is 1.30

that means 1.30 : 1

Interpretation

the gas on top effuses 1.30 times as fast as the gas on the bottom

Worked problem: A mixture of O₂ and Kr is placed in an effusion apparatus. Calculate rate(O₂)rate(Kr).

Step 1 — Predict

O₂ is lighter than Kr, so O₂ should effuse faster.

Step 2 — Direction check

Because O₂ is in the numerator and should be faster, the final ratio should be greater than 1.

rate(O₂)rate(Kr) = √M(Kr)M(O₂)
= √83.80 g/mol32.00 g/mol
rate(O₂)rate(Kr) = 1.62 = 1.62 : 1
Meaning: O₂ effuses 1.62 times as fast as Kr. Because O₂ is in the numerator, the ratio describes how much faster O₂ is than Kr.

Worked Graham’s Law — Find One Gas’s Effusion Rate from Another

Learning Objective: Use a Graham’s-law ratio to calculate an actual effusion rate for a second gas.
Constructed practice: At the same T and P, O₂ effuses at 12.0 mL/min. What is the effusion rate of He?
compare the two gases→ find the ratio→ use the known rate
Prediction: He is lighter, so He should effuse faster than O₂.
rate(He)rate(O₂) = √32.004.003 = 2.83
2.83 = 2.83 : 1 means He effuses 2.83 times as fast as O₂.
rate(He) = (2.83)(12.0 mL/min)
rate(He) = 34.0 mL/min
Meaning: once you know how many times faster He effuses, multiply the known O₂ rate by that factor.

Real Gases — When and Why the Ideal Model Fails

Learning Objectives: Relate ideal-gas assumptions to the conditions that make real gases behave more or less ideally. Distinguish how intermolecular attractions and finite particle volume each make real gases deviate from ideal behavior.

Ideal-gas assumptions

1) particles have negligible volume
2) attractions are negligible

So gases are most ideal when…

particles are far apart and moving enough that attractions matter very little.

Low pressure

particles far apart

High temperature

faster particles, attractions less important

Result

behavior is closer to ideal

High pressure

particles crowded

Low temperature

attractions matter more

Result

greater deviation from ideal

ACS-style decision rule: the least ideal gas behavior is expected under high P + low T, especially for gases with stronger intermolecular attractions or larger particles.
Exam trap: do not memorize only “high P, low T.” Also ask whether the gas itself has stronger attractions than the alternatives.

Cause 1 — Intermolecular attractions

Molecules attract one another, so some particles are pulled slightly away from a direct wall impact.

Effect: the observed pressure can be lower than the ideal prediction when attractions are important.

Cause 2 — Finite particle volume

Real particles occupy space, so the gas has less truly free volume than the ideal model assumes.

Effect: at very high pressure, finite particle volume can make the observed pressure higher than the ideal-gas prediction.

Attractions dominate

Lower T and higher P make intermolecular attractions more important, often lowering observed pressure below the ideal prediction.

Finite volume dominates

At very high P, particle size is no longer negligible and can push observed pressure above the ideal prediction.

Ask which ideal assumption fails: negligible attractions, negligible particle volume, or both.

Easy

Medium

Which sample should behave more ideally: CO₂ at 1 atm and 500 K, or CO₂ at 25 atm and 250 K?

Easy reasoning

same T → same average translational KE

lighter Ne → faster average speed

Medium reasoning

Low P and high T push behavior closer to ideal. High P and low T push behavior away from ideal.

Average translational KE: equal
Average speed: Ne > Xe
More ideal: CO₂ at 1 atm and 500 K

It has lower pressure and higher temperature, so particles are farther apart and attractions matter less.
