Why Use a Calorimeter? — Measure the Surroundings to Infer q of the System

Learning Objective: Explain why calorimetry often determines qsystem indirectly from a measurable temperature change in the surroundings.
We usually do not read q directly from the reaction or object itself. Instead, we observe how the surroundings respond.
What we measure: the temperature change, ΔT, of water, solution, or the calorimeter.
qsurroundings = mcΔT   or   qcalorimeter = CcalΔT
qsystem = −qsurroundings
Same magnitude, opposite direction → opposite signs.
Why this matters: calorimetry usually measures the temperature change of the surroundings first. We calculate qsurroundings, then infer qsystem from the opposite sign.

How the Surroundings Reveal q — Heat Capacity and Specific Heat

Learning Objective: Use heat capacity or specific heat capacity to convert a measured temperature change into q for the surroundings.

Heat Capacity, C

Property of a whole object or calorimeter.

q = CΔT

Typical unit: J/°C or J/K

Specific Heat Capacity, c

Property normalized per gram of substance.

q = mcΔT

Typical unit: J/(g·°C)

Sample warms

Tfinal > Tinitial

ΔT > 0 → q > 0

heat enters the sample

Sample cools

Tfinal < Tinitial

ΔT < 0 → q < 0

heat leaves the sample

High-frequency error: ΔT is never just the final temperature. Always calculate Tfinal − Tinitial.
Fast check: if your sample warms but your calculated q is negative, stop and inspect ΔT or your system definition.

Read the Temperature Change Correctly — q = mcΔT

Learning Objective: Calculate ΔT correctly and use its sign to interpret whether the surroundings absorb or release heat.
Concrete picture: place a hot object into cooler water inside an insulated calorimeter. Heat flows from the hotter object to the cooler water until thermal equilibrium is reached.
General energy balance: in an ideally insulated calorimeter, heat lost by one part is gained by another part.
General form: qlost + qgained = 0
Rearranged: qlost = −qgained
For this problem: qhot object + qwater = 0
Because the hot object is the system: qlost by system = qhot object = −qgained by surroundings = −qwater
General equation first, then the problem-specific labels.
Always name the parts. In this worked example, the system = hot solid and the surroundings = water.

Two Common Calorimetry Conditions — Constant Pressure vs. Constant Volume

Learning Objective: Compare coffee-cup calorimetry at approximately constant pressure with bomb calorimetry at constant volume.
Why control conditions? We want a clean connection between measured heat transfer and the energy quantity of interest.

Coffee-cup calorimeter

approximately constant pressure

often used for solution reactions

qp = ΔH

Preview: enthalpy, H, is developed in T6.3.

Bomb calorimeter

rigid, sealed vessel

constant volume

ΔV = 0 → wPV = 0

qv = ΔE

Condition lock: qp = ΔH and qv = ΔE are not interchangeable. The controlled condition matters.
What happens to temperature? The system and surroundings usually begin at different temperatures, so heat flows between them.
Tinitial, system ≠ Tinitial, surroundings
↓ heat transfer ↓
Tfinal, system = Tfinal, surroundings
Thermal equilibrium: the state in which the system and surroundings have reached the same temperature, so there is no net heat transfer between them.
Do not confuse the conditions. Constant pressure or constant volume describes the calorimeter condition. Thermal equilibrium describes the final temperature relationship between the interacting parts.

Constant-Volume Calorimetry — Bomb Calorimeter Calculation

Learning Objective: Use calorimeter heat capacity and temperature change to determine qcal, qrxn, and ΔE at constant volume.
Worked Example
A food sample is burned in a bomb calorimeter. The temperature rises from 22.9 °C to 32.7 °C. If Ccal = 8.151 kJ/K, determine the heat absorbed by the calorimeter and the energy change of the reaction. Then compare the released energy with 25 Food Calories.
Path: ΔT → qcal = CcalΔT → qrxn = −qcal → constant V: ΔE = qv

1. Temperature change
ΔT = 32.7 − 22.9 = +9.8 K

2. Calorimeter heat
qcal = (8.151 kJ/K)(+9.8 K) = +79.9 kJ

3. Reaction heat
qrxn = −qcal = −79.9 kJ

Rigid bomb calorimeter: ΔV = 0 → wPV = 0 → ΔE = qv = −79.9 kJ
79.9 kJ ÷ 4.184 kJ/Cal = 19.1 Cal. This is below 25 Food Calories.
Do not report ΔH from this measurement. A rigid bomb calorimeter directly gives constant-volume heat, qv, which equals ΔE when PV work is the only work mode.

Worked Example Part 1 — Thermal Equilibrium, System, and q for the Water

Learning Objective: Use thermal equilibrium to identify the common final temperature, keep the hot solid as the system, and calculate qwater.
Thermal equilibrium: heat flows from the hotter object to the cooler water until both reach the same final temperature. At that point there is no net heat transfer between them.
Tfinal, solid = Tfinal, water = 42.8 °C
Energy balance: qsystem + qsurroundings = 0
For this problem: qhot solid + qwater = 0
Useful calculation form: qhot solid = −qwater
Problem: 23.6 g of a hot solid at 178.0 °C is placed into 78 mL of water at 23.0 °C. The final temperature becomes 42.8 °C. Find the specific heat capacity of the solid.

Concept side

system = hot solid

surroundings = water

  • The solid starts hotter, so it loses heat.
  • The water starts cooler, so it gains heat.
  • qhot object + qwater = 0
  • So qhot object = −qwater
ΔTwater = 42.8 − 23.0 = +19.8 °C
ΔTsolid = 42.8 − 178.0 = −135.2 °C

Water calculation side

Use water first because its mass and specific heat are known.

mwater = 78 mL ≈ 78 g
ΔTwater = 42.8 − 23.0 = +19.8 °C
water warms → qwater > 0
Calculation strategy: calculate qwater completely first because mwater, cwater, and ΔTwater are known. Then transfer that value to the system using qhot solid = −qwater.

Concept side

Known first: qwater
Then: qhot object = −qwater
Last: solve for csolid

Water calculation side

qwater = mwatercwaterΔTwater
(78 g)× 4.184 Jg·°C ×(+19.8 °C)

Concept side

Interpretation: the surroundings, water, absorb heat. Because the system = hot solid, the system must lose exactly that amount of heat.

Water calculation side

qwater = +6.46 × 10³ J
Reasonableness: the water warms, so qwater must be positive.

Therefore qhot object = [ _______ ].

qhot solid = −qwater = −(+6.46 × 10³ J) = −6.5 × 10³ J
Water gains +6.5 kJ → the system loses 6.46 kJ.

Worked Example Part 2 — Use the System Temperature Change to Solve for c of the Solid

Learning Objective: Use qhot object = −qwater and the system temperature change to determine the specific heat capacity of the solid.
Start from conservation, then use the convenient form:
qhot solid + qwater = 0  →  qhot solid = −qwater  →  qhot solid = −6.5 × 10³ J
Thermal-equilibrium checkpoint: both the system and surroundings use the same final temperature, 42.8 °C, but each has its own initial temperature and therefore its own ΔT.

Surroundings

water

qwater = +6.46 × 10³ J

⇄

System

hot solid

qhot object = −6.5 × 10³ J

ΔTsystem = ΔTsolid = 42.8 − 178.0 = −135.2 °C
Use the system value directly: qsystem = qhot solid = −6.5 × 10³ J
Use the system values: qhot object, msolid, and ΔTsolid.
qsolid = msolidcsolidΔTsolid
csolid = qsolid / (msolidΔTsolid)
−6.5 × 10³ J(23.6 g)(−135.2 °C)
csolid = 2.03 J/(g·°C)
Sign check: specific heat capacity is positive. The negative q and negative ΔT cancel, as they should for a cooling object.
Common error: do not switch systems halfway through. Once the hot solid is chosen as the system, keep using the system q and the system ΔT for the final calculation.

Guided Transfer Example — Find the Final Temperature at Thermal Equilibrium

Learning Objective: Solve a calorimetry problem in which the shared final temperature is unknown by applying qsystem + qsurroundings = 0.
Guided problem: A 32.5 g aluminum cube at 45.8 °C is submerged in 105.3 g of water at 15.4 °C. Assume the aluminum and water are thermally isolated from everything else. Find the final temperature at thermal equilibrium. Use cAl = 0.903 J/(g·°C) and cwater = 4.184 J/(g·°C).

System

Aluminum

starts hotter → loses heat

Surroundings

Water

starts cooler → gains heat

Before algebra

15.4 °C < Tf < 45.8 °C

Tf should be closer to 15.4 °C because the water has the larger heat capacity.

Use the heat-transfer relationship directly: at thermal equilibrium, heat lost by the aluminum equals the negative of the heat gained by the water.
qAl = −qwater

Aluminum — system

qAl = mAlcAlΔTAl

qAl = (32.5 g)(0.903 J/(g·°C))(Tf − 45.8 °C)

Water — surroundings

qwater = mwatercwaterΔTwater

qwater = (105.3 g)(4.184 J/(g·°C))(Tf − 15.4 °C)

(32.5)(0.903)(Tf − 45.8) = −(105.3)(4.184)(Tf − 15.4)
29.35(Tf − 45.8) = −440.58(Tf − 15.4)
29.35Tf − 1344.1 = −440.58Tf + 6784.9
469.93Tf = 8129.0
Tf = 17.3 °C
Reasonableness: 17.3 °C lies between 15.4 °C and 45.8 °C and is much closer to the water's initial temperature because the water has the larger total heat capacity.

From Calorimetry to Dissolution — Apply the Same Energy Balance to Solutions

Learning Objective: Extend calorimetry to dissolving processes by connecting particle interactions, temperature change, and the sign of ΔHsoln.

Step 1 — Separate solute particles

Existing solute–solute attractions must be overcome.

Energy required

ΔH₁ > 0

Step 2 — Separate solvent particles

Some solvent–solvent attractions must be overcome.

Energy required

ΔH₂ > 0

Step 3 — Form new attractions

New solute–solvent attractions form.

Energy released

ΔH₃ < 0

ΔHsoln = (+ energy required) + (+ energy required) + (− energy released)
What matters is the balance between the energy required to separate particles and the energy released when new attractions form.

Release wins

more energy released than required

ΔHsoln < 0

exothermic dissolution

Required energy wins

more energy required than released

ΔHsoln > 0

endothermic dissolution

Worked Problem 1 — Endothermic Dissolution

Learning Objective: Calculate the heat absorbed by a dissolution process from a measured temperature decrease.
Problem: 3.21 g NH₄NO₃ dissolves in 50.0 g water at 24.9 °C. The final temperature is 20.3 °C. Find qdissolution.
Assume csoln = 4.184 J/(g·°C) and no heat is absorbed by the calorimeter.
Predict first: solution cools → qdissolution should be positive.
1. Mass of solution
msoln = 50.0 g + 3.21 g = 53.21 g
2. Temperature change
ΔTsoln = 20.3 °C − 24.9 °C = −4.6 °C
3. Heat change of solution
qsoln = (53.21 g)(4.184 J/(g·°C))(−4.6 °C) = −1.02 × 10³ J
qdissolution = −qsoln = +1.02 × 10³ J
Reasonableness check: the solution became colder, so it lost thermal energy. The dissolving process must have absorbed that energy → positive q.

Worked Problem 2 — Exothermic Dissolution

Learning Objective: Calculate the heat released by dissolving CaCl₂ and convert the result to molar heat of solution.
Problem: 3.0 g CaCl₂ dissolves in 150.0 g water at 22.4 °C. The final temperature is 25.8 °C. Assume the resulting solution has c = 4.18 J/(g·°C). Find qdissolution and ΔHsoln in kJ/mol CaCl₂.
Predict first: solution warms → qdissolution should be negative.
1. Mass of solution
msoln = 150.0 g + 3.0 g = 153.0 g
2. Temperature change
ΔTsoln = 25.8 °C − 22.4 °C = +3.4 °C
3. Heat gained by solution
qsoln = (153.0 g)(4.18 J/(g·°C))(+3.4 °C) = +2.17 × 10³ J
4. Heat of dissolution for this sample
qdissolution = −2.17 × 10³ J = −2.2 kJ
5. Convert CaCl₂ to moles
3.0 g CaCl₂ × 1 mol CaCl₂110.98 g CaCl₂ = 0.027 mol CaCl₂
6. Molar heat of solution
−2.2 kJ × 10.027 mol CaCl₂ = −80 kJ/mol CaCl₂
Check: negative ΔH matches the observed temperature increase.

Heat of Dilution — Why Can Adding More Solvent Change Enthalpy?

Learning Objective: Explain why an already-existing solution can absorb or release heat when its concentration changes.

More concentrated solution

lower water level
+ solvent →

More dilute solution

higher water level
In a concentrated solution, each solute particle experiences a different local solvent environment than it does in a much more dilute solution. Adding solvent changes these interactions and therefore can change the enthalpy.
concentrated solution + solvent → more dilute solution    ΔHdil
Heat of dilution is an energy change caused by changing concentration—not by adding new solute.
As dilution continues, the heat effect can become smaller. At sufficiently great dilution, adding still more solvent produces essentially no additional heat effect.

Heat of Solution vs. Heat of Dilution — Same Thermochemistry, Different Initial State

Learning Objective: Distinguish the two processes and predict heat flow from qualitative temperature observations.

Heat of Solution

Initial state: solute + solvent

Process: solute dissolves

Final state: solution

Heat of Dilution

Initial state: existing solution + more solvent

Process: concentration decreases

Final state: more dilute solution

Practice — Identify the Process

A. Solid CaCl₂ is added to water and dissolves.

B. Water is added to a concentrated CaCl₂(aq) solution.

Which is heat of solution? Which is heat of dilution?

A: heat of solution    |    B: heat of dilution

Reasoning — Temperature Observation

Water is added to a concentrated solution in an insulated cup and the temperature rises.

What can you conclude about ΔHdil?

The solution gained heat, so the dilution process released heat. Therefore ΔHdil < 0 for that dilution step.
M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ tracks concentration. ΔHdil tracks energy. Do not substitute one idea for the other.
