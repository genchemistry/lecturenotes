From Light to Electrons — de Broglie’s Question

Learning Objective: Recall what we learned about light and explain the new question de Broglie asked about electrons.
Recall from T7.2:
Light has wave-like behavior, but it can also act like a particle when it transfers energy in discrete packets called photons.
We already think of an electron as a tiny moving piece of matter: it has mass, it can have momentum, and when it is detected, it is detected at a specific location.
Later in this topic, we will call these particle-like properties.
de Broglie asked:
If light can behave like a particle, could a moving electron also show wave-like behavior?
He proposed that a moving particle, such as an electron, can be associated with a wavelength.

That wavelength is the de Broglie wavelength.

de Broglie Wavelength — A Wavelength Associated with a Moving Particle

Learning Objective: Define de Broglie wavelength before using the equation and predict how mass and speed affect it.
A moving particle, such as an electron, can be associated with a wave. The wavelength of that matter wave is called the de Broglie wavelength.
This does not mean the electron is literally a tiny water wave. It means its behavior can show measurable wave-like effects.

Worked Problem — de Broglie Wavelength of an Electron

Learning Objective: Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of an electron and check whether the scale is physically reasonable.
Problem: An electron moves at 2.65 × 106 m/s. What de Broglie wavelength is associated with this moving electron?
mass + speed→ momentum, mv→ λ = h/(mv)→ wavelength
Given
h = 6.626 × 10−34 J·s
me = 9.11 × 10−31 kg
v = 2.65 × 106 m/s
Set up
λ = 6.626 × 10−34 J·s (9.11 × 10−31 kg)(2.65 × 106 m/s)
Unit check
Since 1 J = 1 kg·m2/s2, λ units = kg·m2/s kg·m/s = m
Calculate
λ = 2.74 × 10−10 m = 0.274 nm
Reasonableness: this wavelength is on an atomic-scale distance, so wave behavior can matter for electrons.

From de Broglie to Quantum Mechanics — Scientific Background

Learning Objective: Place de Broglie, Schrödinger, and Heisenberg in the scientific development that replaced the classical electron-orbit picture.
de Broglie’s matter-wave idea changed the question: if an electron can have wave properties, then a fixed classical orbit may not be the best way to describe it.
1924

de Broglie

Proposed that moving particles can have an associated wavelength.

→
1926

Schrödinger

Developed a wave equation for describing allowed electron states.

→
1927

Heisenberg

Showed that exact position and exact momentum cannot both be specified simultaneously.

Together, these ideas moved atomic theory away from electrons traveling on exact classical paths and toward a quantum-mechanical description.
We only need the conceptual background here. The formal quantum numbers and orbital details come next.

Schrödinger — From Electron Waves to Wavefunctions

Learning Objective: Explain conceptually how Schrödinger used a wave-based mathematical description to represent allowed electron states.
de Broglie proposed that electrons have wave properties. Schrödinger asked: Can we build a mathematical wave description for an electron in an atom?
ψ

Wavefunction

The Schrödinger equation gives allowed wavefunctions and their corresponding energy states.

In General Chemistry I, we do not solve the full Schrödinger equation. We use what its solutions tell us about electron states and orbitals.

Not a fixed orbit

We do not assign the electron one exact circular path around the nucleus.

Probability description

ψ² is related to the probability of finding the electron in a region of space.

This is the foundation of the modern orbital picture.

Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle — Why an Exact Electron Path Fails

Learning Objective: Explain why exact position and exact momentum cannot both be specified simultaneously and why this undermines a fixed electron trajectory.
Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle:
The exact position and exact momentum of an electron cannot both be known at the same time.

Important: this is not simply a problem caused by weak instruments. The uncertainty is part of the quantum description itself.
Therefore, an electron cannot be treated like a tiny planet moving on a perfectly known path.

Electron: Particle-Like, Wave-Like, and Quantum-Mechanical

Learning Objective: Synthesize the evidence and models that lead from the familiar particle picture to the modern quantum description.

Particle-Like

Electron has mass and momentum.

It is detected at a specific location.

Wave-Like

Electron has a de Broglie wavelength.

It can produce wave-like patterns.

Schrödinger: describe electron states with wavefunctions and probability.
Heisenberg: do not assign the electron a perfectly known position and momentum at the same time.
T7.3 Takeaway:
The electron is not adequately described as a tiny particle traveling on a fixed orbit. Modern chemistry uses a quantum-mechanical probability description.
