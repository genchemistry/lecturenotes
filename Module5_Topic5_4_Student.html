One Gas, Two States — Derive the Relationship

Learning Objectives: Represent the initial and final conditions of the same gas sample with two ideal-gas equations. Use the two ideal-gas equations to remove quantities that are identical in both states.
New situation: the same sample of gas changes from an initial state to a final state. Some of P, V, n, and T may change; others may stay constant.

Same sample?

Then the amount of gas does not change: n₁ = n₂.

Same equation?

R is a constant, so the same R appears in both states.

Do not choose a memorized gas-law formula first. First ask: what changed, and what stayed constant?
Cancel the quantities that are unchanged:
P₁V₁/T₁ = nR = P₂V₂/T₂
Because the same gas sample has the same n and R, both states equal the same constant nR.
P₁V₁T₁ = P₂V₂T₂
This is not another formula to memorize. It comes from writing PV=nRT for the two states and removing the common quantities.

General Two-State Calculation — P, V, and T All Change

Learning Objective: Solve a two-state gas problem when no additional P, V, or T variable stays constant.
Problem: A gas sample occupies 2.50 L at 1.20 atm and 300 K. It changes to 2.00 L and 330 K. What is the final pressure?

Initial State

P₁ = 1.20 atm
V₁ = 2.50 L
same n
T₁ = 300 K
→

Final State

P₂ = ?
V₂ = 2.00 L
same n
T₂ = 330 K
Constant check: n is constant because it is the same gas sample. P, V, and T all change, so none of them can be canceled.
Start with the general two-state relationship:
P₁V₁T₁ = P₂V₂T₂
Solve for the actual unknown:
P₂ = P₁V₁T₂T₁V₂
P₂ = (1.20 atm)(2.50 L)(330 K)(300 K)(2.00 L) = 1.65 atm
Reasonableness: the gas is compressed and warmed. Both changes tend to increase pressure, so the final pressure should be above 1.20 atm. It is.

Rigid Container Calculation — V Is Constant

Learning Objective: Recognize constant volume in a sealed rigid container, cancel V, and solve the reduced relationship.
Problem: A sealed rigid container is at 0.950 atm and 20.0 °C. It is heated to 80.0 °C. Find the final pressure.

Start: P₁V₁T₁ = P₂V₂T₂
Rigid container → V₁ = V₂. Cancel V.
P₁T₁ = P₂T₂
Solve for P₂:   P₂ = P₁ × T₂T₁
T₁ = 20.0 + 273.15 = 293.15 K    T₂ = 80.0 + 273.15 = 353.15 K
P₂ = 0.950 atm × 353.15 K293.15 K = 1.144 atm ≈ 1.14 atm
Reasonableness: the temperature increased while volume stayed fixed, so pressure should increase. It does.

Same T and P — Gas Volume Can Follow the Coefficients Directly

Learning Objective: Use stoichiometric coefficients as gas-volume ratios only when the gases are measured at identical temperature and pressure.
N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) → 2 NH₃(g)
same T and P → 1 L N₂ : 3 L H₂ : 2 L NH₃
4.00 L N₂ × 3 L H₂1 L N₂ = 12.0 L H₂
4.00 L N₂ × 2 L NH₃1 L N₂ = 8.00 L NH₃
If T or P differs: do not use liters as the reaction ratio. Convert through moles first.

Worked Example — Gas Data to Reactant Mass

Learning Objective: Use measured PVT data for SiF₄ to determine the mass of SiO₂ consumed.
SiO₂(s) + 4 HF(g) → SiF₄(g) + 2 H₂O(l)
Given: 115 mL SiF₄ at 32.0 °C and 0.879 atm. M(SiO₂) = 60.1 g/mol.
mL SiF₄→L SiF₄→mol SiF₄→mol SiO₂→g SiO₂
115 mL SiF₄ × 1 L1000 mL = 0.115 L SiF₄
T = 32.0 + 273.15 = 305.15 K
n(SiF₄) = PVRT = (0.879 atm)(0.115 L)(0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(305.15 K) = 0.00404 mol SiF₄
0.00404 mol SiF₄ × 1 mol SiO₂1 mol SiF₄ × 60.1 g SiO₂1 mol SiO₂ = 0.243 g SiO₂
Reasonableness: 0.115 L gas near 1 atm corresponds to only a few millimoles, so a sub-gram reactant mass is reasonable.

Worked Example — Reactant Mass to Gas Product Volume

Learning Objective: Convert glucose mass to CO₂ moles, then use the actual gas conditions to determine CO₂ volume.
C₆H₁₂O₆(s) + 6 O₂(g) → 6 CO₂(g) + 6 H₂O(l)
Given: 5.60 g C₆H₁₂O₆ is consumed. Find V(CO₂) at 37.0 °C and 1.00 atm. M(C₆H₁₂O₆) = 180 g/mol.
g glucose→mol glucose→mol CO₂→V = nRT/P
5.60 g C₆H₁₂O₆ × 1 mol C₆H₁₂O₆180 g C₆H₁₂O₆ × 6 mol CO₂1 mol C₆H₁₂O₆ = 0.1867 mol CO₂

Reverse Transfer + Method Selection

Learning Objectives: Work backward from gas PVT data through a mole ratio to the mass of a solid reactant. Choose among PV=nRT, STP molar volume, and same-T/P gas-volume ratios from the conditions.
2 KClO₃(s) → 2 KCl(s) + 3 O₂(g)
Constructed transfer problem: 1.85 L O₂ is produced at 27.0 °C and 0.975 atm. What mass of KClO₃ decomposed? M(KClO₃) = 122.55 g/mol.
P,V,T O₂→mol O₂→mol KClO₃→g KClO₃
T = 27.0 + 273.15 = 300.15 K
n(O₂) = (0.975 atm)(1.85 L)(0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(300.15 K) = 0.0732 mol O₂
0.0732 mol O₂ × 2 mol KClO₃3 mol O₂ × 122.55 g KClO₃1 mol KClO₃ = 5.98 g KClO₃
Ratio check: 3 mol O₂ comes from 2 mol KClO₃, so the KClO₃ mole amount must be smaller than the O₂ mole amount.

Actual P and T given

Use PV=nRT.

Explicitly at STP

Use 22.4 L/mol if helpful.

Two reacting gases at same T and P

Use coefficient volume ratios.

Fast check: conditions decide the gas conversion. The balanced equation decides the stoichiometric ratio.
Do not combine shortcuts casually. If the conditions do not satisfy a shortcut, return to moles and PV=nRT.

Worked ACS-Style Practice — Gas Stoichiometry

Learning Objective: Apply STP molar volume, same-T/P volume ratios, and full stoichiometry + PV=nRT in increasing difficulty.
Easy

STP

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. If 3.00 mol H₂ reacts with excess N₂, what NH₃ volume forms at STP?

3.00 mol H₂ × 2 mol NH₃3 mol H₂ × 22.4 L NH₃1 mol NH₃ = 44.8 L NH₃
Medium

Same T and P

For N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, what H₂ volume reacts with 5.00 L N₂ when both are measured at the same T and P?

5.00 L N₂ × 3 L H₂1 L N₂ = 15.0 L H₂
Hard

Stoichiometry + PVT

2Al + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂. Find H₂ volume from 4.50 g Al at 24 °C and 0.950 atm.

4.50 g Al × 1 mol Al26.98 g Al × 3 mol H₂2 mol Al = 0.2502 mol H₂
T = 24 + 273.15 = 297.15 K
V = nRTP = 6.42 L H₂

Wrong shortcut

22.4 L/mol at 24 °C → not STP.

Wrong mole ratio

1:1 Al:H₂ → ignores 2:3 coefficients.

Wrong temperature

24 used directly → must convert to Kelvin.

Balanced equation → moles → mole ratio → requested gas quantity
