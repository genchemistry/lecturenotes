From Hess Law to Standard Enthalpy — Build a Common Reference System

Learning Objective: Connect Hess Law and state-function reasoning to standard-state enthalpies, then interpret the degree symbol in ΔH° and ΔH°f.
Transition from T6.5: Hess Law showed that enthalpy depends only on the initial and final states. Standard enthalpies of formation now give us a common reference system for applying that idea systematically. We already know that ΔH belongs to a reaction as written. But tabulated enthalpy values must be measured relative to a consistent reference condition.
ΔH   vs.   ΔH°
The superscript ° means the quantity refers to standard-state conditions.
Pressure 1 atm
Temperature 25.0 °C
Solutions 1 M
Course convention: these are the standard conditions used in our General Chemistry I thermochemistry materials.
Do not confuse standard state with Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP). The ° symbol here is a thermodynamic reference-state notation, not the gas-law STP shortcut.

Standard Enthalpy of Formation, ΔH°f — Build Exactly 1 mol

Learning Objective: Define standard enthalpy of formation and identify the two requirements of a valid formation reaction.
Standard enthalpy of formation, ΔH°f: the enthalpy change when exactly 1 mol of a substance is formed from its constituent elements in their standard states.
elements in standard states→ 1 mol compound
C(s, graphite) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)    ΔH°f = −393.5 kJ/mol
Why this qualifies: carbon and oxygen are elements in their standard states, and the equation forms exactly 1 mol CO₂(g).
Formation equation checklist: 1) product coefficient must be 1; 2) reactants must be elements, not compounds; 3) use the correct standard state of each element; 4) fractions are allowed if needed to make exactly 1 mol product.

A formation equation must produce exactly [ _______ ] of product.

Which ΔH°f Values Equal Zero? — Element + Standard State

Learning Objective: Identify species whose standard enthalpy of formation is zero and reject common look-alike choices.
Rule: ΔH°f = 0 for an element in its standard state.

ΔH°f = 0

H₂(g) N₂(g) O₂(g) Br₂(l) C(s, graphite)

Not zero by this rule

H(g) O₃(g) C(s, diamond) H₂O(l) CO₂(g)
Think of ΔH°f as a height measured from a chosen zero level. We need one common starting point so that the formation enthalpies of different substances can be compared.
reference level: ΔH°f = 0
O₂(g), elemental oxygen in its standard state
compounds can lie above or below this reference
Why is O₂(g) assigned ΔH°f = 0?

The definition of ΔH°f asks: How much enthalpy change is required to form a substance from its elements in their standard states?
O₂(g, standard state) → O₂(g, standard state)
The starting reference and the final substance are the same state.
There is no formation change to measure → ΔH°f = 0.
Important: this does NOT mean O₂ has zero energy or zero enthalpy. It means we choose its standard-state formation enthalpy as the zero reference, just as sea level can be chosen as zero elevation.

Writing a Formation Equation — Do It in Three Steps

Learning Objective: Write a standard formation equation step by step by fixing 1 mol product, selecting elemental standard states, and then balancing.
Example: Write the standard formation equation for Na₂CO₃(s).
Step 1 — Write exactly 1 mol of product
→ Na₂CO₃(s)

Do not change this product coefficient from 1.

Step 2 — Choose each element in its standard state
Na(s) + C(s, graphite) + O₂(g) → Na₂CO₃(s)
We use Na(s), C(s, graphite), and O₂(g) because the reactants of a formation equation must be the constituent elements in their standard states.
Step 3 — Balance while keeping the product coefficient = 1
2 Na(s) + C(s, graphite) + 3/2 O₂(g) → Na₂CO₃(s)

Another example: AgCl(s)

Ag(s) + 1/2 Cl₂(g) → AgCl(s)

Another example: NH₃(g)

1/2 N₂(g) + 3/2 H₂(g) → NH₃(g)

Fractions are allowed. The priority is exactly 1 mol of product.

From ΔH°f to ΔH°rxn — Compare the Two Sides of the Reaction

Learning Objective: Explain why formation enthalpies can be combined to obtain the standard enthalpy change of an entire reaction.
What do we already know? Each ΔH°f tells us the enthalpy change required to place 1 mol of a substance at its enthalpy level starting from the same elemental standard-state reference.
Reactant side
Add the formation enthalpies needed to represent all reactants.

Product side
Add the formation enthalpies needed to represent all products.
What do we want? ΔH°rxn is the standard enthalpy change for the whole balanced reaction. Since both sides are measured from the same elemental reference baseline, the reaction change is simply the difference between the two totals.
overall reaction change = total enthalpy of products − total enthalpy of reactants
ΔH°rxn = Σ νΔH°f(products) − Σ νΔH°f(reactants)

Why coefficients matter: ΔH°f is per 1 mol, so the balanced-equation coefficient tells how many formation-enthalpy units to count.

Worked Problem — Calculate ΔH°rxn from ΔH°f Values

Learning Objective: Calculate a standard reaction enthalpy using a products-minus-reactants ledger and interpret its sign.
Problem: Calculate ΔH°rxn for the combustion of methane:
CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l)

CH₄(g)

ΔH°f = −74.8 kJ/mol

O₂(g)

ΔH°f = 0

CO₂(g)

ΔH°f = −393.5 kJ/mol

H₂O(l)

ΔH°f = −285.8 kJ/mol

Products

1 CO₂1(−393.5)
2 H₂O(l)2(−285.8)
Σ products = −965.1 kJ

Reactants

1 CH₄1(−74.8)
2 O₂2(0)
Σ reactants = −74.8 kJ
ΔH°rxn = (−965.1 kJ) − (−74.8 kJ) = −890.3 kJ
Yes — the negative sign tells us the reaction is exothermic.
ΔH°rxn < 0 means the products are at a lower enthalpy than the reactants. The enthalpy difference is released to the surroundings as heat under constant-pressure conditions.
Interpretation: for the methane combustion reaction as written, about 890.3 kJ of heat is released.
Quick check: O₂(g) contributes 0 because it is elemental oxygen in its standard state.
