Acid–Base Reactions — What Must Be Memorized?

Learning Objective: Recognize strong acids, strong bases, weak bases, and the core neutralization reaction in aqueous solution.

Brønsted Acid

proton (H⁺) donor

Brønsted Base

proton (H⁺) acceptor

Neutralization

H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O

the core chemical change

Common strong acids to memorize

HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, HClO₃, HClO₄

H₂SO₄: strong for its first ionization; the second ionization is weaker.

Strong bases to recognize

Group 1 hydroxides: LiOH, NaOH, KOH, RbOH, CsOH

Soluble Group 2 hydroxides often emphasized: Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂

Weak-base note: NH₃(aq) is a weak base. “NH₄OH” is often used as an intro-level shorthand for aqueous ammonia, but the solution is better represented as NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻.
Solubility chart link: many hydroxides are insoluble; soluble hydroxides are the strong bases commonly used in neutralization problems.

Strong vs. Weak — What Changes in the Net Ionic Equation?

Learning Objective: Distinguish strong and weak acid/base behavior, predict what remains in the net ionic equation, and connect neutralization to prior ionic-equation reasoning.
Strong acid + strong base
HCl = strong acid; NaOH = strong base.
Both are represented as ions in the complete ionic equation.
H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l)
Weak acid + strong base
HF = weak acid; NaOH = strong base.
HF is kept intact before the reaction.
HF(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) + F⁻(aq)
HF(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) + F⁻(aq)
HF gives its H to OH⁻ → H₂O forms, and F⁻ remains.
KEEP does not mean “does not react.” It means HF is not written as fully ionized before the reaction because HF is a weak acid.
Strength: degree of ionization or dissociation.
Concentration: amount of solute in a given volume.
Common misconception: dilute does not mean weak. A dilute HCl solution is still a strong acid.

Precipitation net ionic

ions → precipitate(s)

shows the solid being formed

Neutralization net ionic

acid + base → water (l)

shows water being formed

Procedure reminder: write the complete ionic equation before canceling spectator ions. Do not jump directly to the net ionic equation.

Weak-Acid Neutralization — HF + NaOH

Learning Objective: Build the complete ionic equation step by step and identify the spectator ion for a weak acid–strong base reaction.
MolecularHF(aq) + NaOH(aq) → H₂O(l) + NaF(aq)
Identify first: HF is a weak acid; NaOH is a strong base.
Key difference: HF is a weak acid, so HF is kept intact when we convert the molecular equation to the complete ionic equation.

The H from HF combines with OH⁻ to form H₂O. F⁻ remains, while Na⁺ is unchanged on both sides.
Spectator ion: Na⁺(aq) appears unchanged on both sides, so it is canceled.

Product Completion Activity — H₂SO₄ + Ba(OH)₂

Learning Objective: Use the outside–outside / inside–inside pattern to complete an acid–base molecular equation.
Guided Activity
H₂SO₄(aq) + Ba(OH)₂(aq) → ?
Identify first: H₂SO₄ is diprotic. Its first ionization is strong, and in this overall neutralization/precipitation reaction both acidic H equivalents are consumed according to the balanced molecular equation. Ba(OH)₂ is a strong base because it is a soluble heavy Group 2 hydroxide.

Outside + outside

H⁺OH⁻H₂O

acid H⁺ combines with base OH⁻ to form water

and

Inside + inside

Ba²⁺SO₄²⁻BaSO₄

remaining ions form the salt; BaSO₄ is insoluble

H₂SO₄(aq) + Ba(OH)₂(aq) → 2H₂O(l) + BaSO₄(s)
Why this is useful: the same partner-swap idea still helps, but acid–base chemistry highlights water formation, and solubility tells us BaSO₄ is a solid.

Harder Stoichiometry — Find Product Mass

Learning Objective: Convert from moles of base to grams of water using the balanced neutralization equation.
Harder Calculation
What mass of H₂O forms when 0.250 mol Mg(OH)₂ is completely neutralized by excess HCl?
Write the balanced equation first.
Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O
0.250 mol Mg(OH)₂ × 2 mol H₂O1 mol Mg(OH)₂ = 0.500 mol H₂O
0.500 mol H₂O × 18.02 g H₂O1 mol H₂O = [ _______ ]
Reasonableness: Mg(OH)₂ has two OH⁻ groups, so 0.250 mol base makes 0.500 mol water.
