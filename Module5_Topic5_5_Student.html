Dalton’s Law — Total Pressure as a Sum of Component Pressures

Learning Objectives: State Dalton’s law and preview how it connects total pressure, mole fraction, and gas collected over water. Apply Dalton’s law conceptually to atmospheric pressure as the sum of the partial pressures of the gases in air.
Dalton’s Law of Partial Pressures: for a mixture of nonreacting gases, each gas contributes a partial pressure and the total is their sum.
Ptotal = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + …
partial pressure = one gas’s contribution to the mixture pressure

1 · Add partial pressures

Use Dalton directly: Ptotal = ΣPi.

2 · Use mole fraction

A gas’s share of the moles gives its share of the total pressure: Pi = XiPtotal.

3 · Gas over water

Water vapor is another gas contribution: Ptotal = Pgas + PH₂O.

One law → three ways we will use it in this Topic.
Connection to Dalton: air is not one gas. Each gas in air contributes part of the atmospheric pressure.
N₂
O₂
other

Air contains mostly N₂ and O₂, with smaller amounts of Ar, CO₂, and other gases.

Patm = PN₂ + PO₂ + PAr + PCO₂ + …

Compare

Each individual partial pressure is less than the total atmospheric pressure.

Big idea

The total does not belong to one gas. It is the sum of the contributions from all gases present.

Mole Fraction → Partial Pressure

Learning Objectives: Calculate mole fraction and interpret it as a gas component’s share of the mixture. Connect mole fraction to Dalton’s law by calculating a component partial pressure from its share of the mixture.
Why mole fraction matters here: Dalton tells us each gas contributes part of the total pressure. Mole fraction tells us what fraction of the mixture belongs to that gas.
Xi = nintotal
0 ≤ Xi ≤ 1

fraction of total moles

ΣXi = 1

all fractions add to one

no units

mol ÷ mol → units cancel

Example: If a mixture has 2.0 mol A and 3.0 mol B, ntotal = 5.0 mol and XA = 2.0 mol A5.0 mol total = 0.40.
Do not use mass fraction. Dalton’s mole-fraction relationship uses moles of gas particles, not grams.
moles of each gas→ntotal→Xi→Pi
Pi = XiPtotal
A gas that makes up a larger mole fraction contributes a larger fraction of the total pressure.

Check 1

ΣXi = 1

Check 2

ΣPi = Ptotal

Check 3

Every Xi and Pi must be positive and smaller than its corresponding total.

Worked Example — Natural Gas Mixture

Learning Objective: Calculate a component partial pressure from its mole fraction.
Given: 8.24 mol CH₄, 0.421 mol C₂H₆, 0.116 mol C₃H₈; Ptotal = 1.37 atm. Find PC₃H₈.
ntotal→XC₃H₈→PC₃H₈
ntotal = 8.24 + 0.421 + 0.116 = 8.777 mol
XC₃H₈ = 0.116 mol C₃H₈8.777 mol total = 0.01322
PC₃H₈ = (0.01322)(1.37 atm) = 0.0181 atm
Reasonableness: propane is only about 1.3% of the moles, so its partial pressure should be only about 1.3% of the total pressure.

Dalton Application 3 — Gas Collected over Water

Learning Objective: Apply Dalton’s law to a gas collected over water by treating water vapor as one component of the gas mixture.
Why this is Dalton’s law: the collection tube contains the target gas and H₂O vapor. Each gas contributes its own partial pressure.
desired gas pressure: Pgas
water-vapor pressure: PH₂O
Ptotal = Pgas + PH₂O
Ptotal = Pgas + PH₂O
Dalton’s law→ solve for target gas→ Pgas = Ptotal − PH₂O
Key sequence: the measured pressure is a mixture pressure. Use Dalton first to remove the water-vapor contribution, then use the dry-gas pressure in PV = nRT.
Dalton first → ideal gas law second.

Worked Gas over Water — Identify an Unknown Metal

Learning Objective: Correct wet-gas pressure, use PV = nRT to find H₂ moles, and infer the metal molar mass.
Reaction: X(s) + 2HCl(aq) → XCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)
Data: 0.0985 g metal; 42.84 mL H₂; 22 °C; Ptotal = 647 torr; PH₂O = 19.8 torr.
wet P→dry H₂ P→mol H₂→mol X→M of X
PH₂ = 647 − 19.8 = 627.2 torr
627.2 torr × 1 atm760 torr = 0.8253 atm
n(H₂) = PVRT = (0.8253 atm)(0.04284 L)(0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(295.15 K) = 1.460 × 10⁻³ mol
1 mol X : 1 mol H₂ → n(X) = 1.460 × 10⁻³ mol
M(X) = 0.0985 g1.460 × 10⁻³ mol = 67.5 g/mol
Closest common metal consistent with X²⁺ chemistry: Zn. The experimental value need not equal the tabulated molar mass exactly.

Worked Gas over Water — Acetylene

Learning Objective: Find the mass of a gas collected over water by correcting its pressure before using the ideal gas law.
Reaction: CaC₂(s) + 2H₂O(l) → C₂H₂(g) + Ca(OH)₂(aq)
Data: Ptotal = 738 torr; PH₂O = 21 torr; V = 523 mL; T = 23 °C.
Pdry→atm, L, K→mol C₂H₂→g C₂H₂
PC₂H₂ = 738 − 21 = 717 torr = 0.9434 atm
V = 0.523 L; T = 296.15 K
n = (0.9434 atm)(0.523 L)(0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(296.15 K) = 0.02030 mol C₂H₂
0.02030 mol C₂H₂ × 26.04 g C₂H₂1 mol C₂H₂ = 0.529 g C₂H₂
Why does the correction matter? The measured 738 torr contains both C₂H₂ and water vapor. If we incorrectly use 738 torr as if it were only C₂H₂, the pressure entered into PV = nRT is too high. That makes the calculated moles of C₂H₂ too high, so the calculated mass is also too high.

Find Ptotal.

Medium — Mole Fraction → Partial Pressure

Find PAr.

0.52 atm + 0.31 atm

(0.0350)(745 torr)

Easy answer

0.83 atm

Medium answer

26.1 torr

Check: 3.50% of 745 torr should be only a few tens of torr.

Problem: H₂ is collected over water at 25 °C. Ptotal = 755 torr and PH₂O = 23.8 torr. Which pressure belongs in PV = nRT when calculating moles of H₂?
Ask what the gauge measures→H₂ + H₂O vapor→remove H₂O contribution
Ptotal = PH₂ + PH₂O
PH₂ = Ptotal − PH₂O
PH₂ = 755 torr − 23.8 torr
PH₂ = 731.2 torr
Why? PV = nRT must use the pressure of the gas whose moles we are calculating. The measured total pressure also includes water vapor.

Experimental Error Transfer — After Dalton’s Law

Learning Objective: Predict how a gas-collection error propagates through measured volume, calculated moles, and a derived molar mass.
Scenario: A metal reacts with acid and produces H₂. The H₂ is collected over water, but the collection tube has a small crack. Some H₂ escapes before the gas volume is measured.

What happens first?

Less H₂ remains in the collection tube.

Measured volume

The recorded H₂ volume is too low.

H₂ escapes→ measured V ↓→ calculated n(H₂) ↓
At the same measured P and T, PV = nRT tells us that a smaller measured gas volume gives a smaller calculated amount of H₂.
n(H₂) too low→ n(metal) too low→ M = mass ÷ mol→ calculated molar mass ↑
Final error direction: if H₂ escapes, the calculated molar mass of the metal is too high.
Do not memorize the arrow chain. Follow the measured quantity first, then trace how that error enters each equation.
