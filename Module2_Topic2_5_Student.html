Cations and Anions

Learning Objective: Distinguish electron loss from electron gain and relate each process to charge sign.

Cation

positive ion
Na → Na+ + e−

Loses one or more electrons.

Na+Mg2+Al3+

Anion

negative ion
Cl + e− → Cl−

Gains one or more electrons.

N3−O2−F−
Common misconception: ions form by gaining or losing electrons, not by gaining or losing protons.

Use the A-group labels.
For this quick charge rule, pay special attention to 1A, 2A, 3A, 5A, 6A, and 7A.
Main-group metals → fixed positive charge
1A → +1, 2A → +2, 3A → +3.
Group number gives the charge magnitude, then add a plus sign.
Transition metals → often variable positive charges
Many transition metals can form more than one positive ion. When a metal has more than one possible charge, its charge is shown with a Roman numeral in the compound name.
Common fixed-charge exceptions you should recognize: Ag+, Zn2+, and Cd2+.
Important main-group exceptions: Sn and Pb are main-group metals, but they commonly form more than one cation: Sn2+ / Sn4+ and Pb2+ / Pb4+.
Main-group nonmetals → common negative charge
5A → −3, 6A → −2, 7A → −1.
Magic number: 8 → A-group number minus 8 gives the charge.
Why 8? We will explain the reason later in Module 8.

Main-group metals
Na, Mg, Al
Na is in 1A → Na+
Mg is in 2A → Mg2+
Al is in 3A → Al3+
Variable positive charges
Fe, Cu, Sn, Pb
Fe commonly forms Fe2+ or Fe3+
Cu commonly forms Cu+ or Cu2+
Sn commonly forms Sn2+ or Sn4+
Pb commonly forms Pb2+ or Pb4+
Remember: not every transition metal is variable-charge. Ag+, Zn2+, and Cd2+ are common fixed-charge cases.
Main-group nonmetals
N, O, Cl
N is in 5A → 5 − 8 = −3 → N3−
O is in 6A → 6 − 8 = −2 → O2−
Cl is in 7A → 7 − 8 = −1 → Cl−
Takeaway: before you write a formula, say the ion charges out loud first. Example: calcium is +2, chloride is −1, so the neutral formula must be CaCl2.

Guided Practice — Counting Electrons in Ions

Learning Objective: Determine the number of electrons in cations and anions from atomic number and ion charge.
IonProtonsChargeElectrons
Ca2+20+2[_______]
N3−7−3[_______]
Al3+13+3[_______]

Cation shortcut

Electrons = protons − charge magnitude

Ca2+: 20 − 2 = 18 e−

Anion shortcut

Electrons = protons + charge magnitude

N3−: 7 + 3 = 10 e−

Check yourself: a positive ion has fewer electrons than protons. A negative ion has more electrons than protons.

Monatomic anions

Name = root + [_______]

O2− → oxide ion

Polyatomic Ions — Use the Course Chart

Learning Objective: Recognize polyatomic ions and use the course chart as a working reference.
Before continuing: download and print the Polyatomic Ion Chart posted in the course shell. Keep it in front of you while watching this video and use it as we work through the examples.
Expectation: I will not cover every polyatomic ion in this lecture. We will focus on common examples, and the chart will serve as your larger reference.

What is a polyatomic ion?

An ion made of two or more atoms acting together as one charged unit.

NO3− nitrate
SO42− sulfate
OH− hydroxide

Do not split it apart

When a polyatomic ion appears in a formula, keep the atoms together as one unit.

Ca(NO3)2
NH4Cl

Read with the chart

Follow nitrate, sulfate, carbonate, hydroxide, and ammonium on the printed chart as they appear in examples.

Preview for T2.7

In compound naming, keep the polyatomic ion name unchanged: calcium nitrate, ammonium sulfate, iron(III) sulfate.

Composite example: Fe2(SO4)3 contains the transition-metal cation Fe3+ and the polyatomic anion sulfate, SO42−.
