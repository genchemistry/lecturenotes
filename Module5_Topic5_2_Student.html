Boyle’s Law — Predict First, Then Calculate

Learning Objectives: Predict and calculate how pressure and volume change when temperature and amount of gas are constant. Use Boyle’s law after predicting the direction of pressure change.

Everyday Example: A Syringe

Close the tip of a syringe and push the plunger inward.

What you feel: the gas becomes harder to compress as the space gets smaller.

What Changes?

Volume decreases while the same gas is trapped inside.

Observation: V ↓ → P ↑

Larger Volume

More space → fewer wall collisions per unit time → lower P

⇄same T
same n
inverse

Smaller Volume

Less space → more wall collisions per unit time → higher P

Boyle’s Law in one picture: when V decreases, P increases; when V increases, P decreases.
At constant T and n:   P ∝ 1V    and    P₁V₁ = P₂V₂
T constantn constant

Pressure and volume change in [ _______ ].

Worked example: O₂(g) occupies 12.0 L at 600. mmHg. What is the new pressure at 36.0 L? T and n are constant.
Predict first: volume triples, so pressure should fall to about one-third.
P₂ = P₁V₁V₂
P₂ = (600. mmHg)(12.0 L)36.0 L = 200. mmHg
Reasonableness: 600. mmHg → 200. mmHg matches the inverse prediction.

Charles’s Law — Kelvin Connects Temperature to Volume

Learning Objectives: Relate gas volume to Kelvin temperature when pressure and amount of gas are constant. Convert Celsius to Kelvin and solve a Charles’s law problem.

Everyday Example: A Balloon

A balloon placed in a warmer place becomes slightly larger; in a colder place, it becomes smaller.

What Changes?

If outside pressure stays about constant, temperature and volume change together.

Observation: T ↑ → V ↑

Colder Gas

lower T

Lower T → smaller V

⇄same P
same n
direct

Warmer Gas

higher T

Higher T → larger V

Charles’s Law in one picture: when T increases, V increases; when T decreases, V decreases, provided P and n stay constant.
At constant P and n:   V ∝ T(K)    and    V₁T₁ = V₂T₂
P constantn constant

Charles’s law requires temperature in [ _______ ].

Worked example: A balloon has a volume of 785 mL at 21 °C. If the temperature drops to 0 °C, what is the new volume? P and n are constant.
Predict: temperature decreases, so volume should decrease.
T₁ = 21 + 273.15 = 294.15 K    T₂ = 0 + 273.15 = 273.15 K
V₂ = V₁ × T₂T₁
V₂ = 785 mL × 273.15 K294.15 K = 729 mL = 0.729 L
Common error: never use 21 and 0 directly in the temperature ratio.

Gay-Lussac’s Law — Heating a Rigid Container

Learning Objectives: Predict and calculate how pressure changes with Kelvin temperature in a rigid container. Use Kelvin temperature to calculate pressure change in a rigid container.

Everyday Example: A Sealed Can in a Hot Car

A sealed rigid container can build up pressure as it gets hotter.

Safety context only: this is an observation, not an experiment to try.

What Changes?

The container volume stays fixed, so temperature and pressure change together.

Observation: T ↑ → P ↑
lower T
higher T
→
✦

Particle Explanation

Heating makes particles move faster. Because the container cannot expand, they strike the walls more often and with greater momentum change.

Microscopic → macroscopic: faster particles + fixed V → higher P.
At constant V and n:   P ∝ T(K)    and    P₁T₁ = P₂T₂
V constantn constant

This law applies when volume is [ _______ ].

A rigid gas container is at 1.00 atm and 25.0 °C. If it is heated to 100.0 °C, what pressure will it reach?
Predict: higher T in a rigid container → higher P.
T₁ = 298.15 K    T₂ = 373.15 K
P₂ = P₁ × T₂T₁
P₂ = 1.00 atm × 373.15 K298.15 K = 1.25 atm
Distractor alert: 10025 is not a valid gas-law temperature ratio.

Avogadro’s Law + STP — Amount, Volume, and a Special Reference Condition

Learning Objectives: Relate gas volume to number of moles when temperature and pressure are constant. Solve an Avogadro’s law problem and recognize the course STP reference.

Everyday Example: Blowing Up a Balloon

Each breath adds more gas particles to the balloon, and the balloon gets larger.

What Changes?

At roughly constant temperature and outside pressure, amount of gas and volume increase together.

Observation: n ↑ → V ↑

Particle Explanation

More gas particles need more space if temperature and pressure are to remain the same.

Microscopic → macroscopic: more particles → larger volume.

Same T and P

If the number of moles doubles, the volume doubles.

At constant T and P:   V ∝ n    and    V₁n₁ = V₂n₂
T constantP constant

Volume is directly proportional to the number of [ _______ ].

Worked example: 0.75 mol He occupies 1.5 L. What volume will 1.2 mol He occupy at the same T and P?
Predict: more moles → larger volume.
V₂ = V₁ × n₂n₁ = 1.5 L × 1.2 mol0.75 mol = 2.4 L
Course STP: 273.15 K and 1 atm → 1 mol ideal gas ≈ 22.4 L
STP lock: 22.4 L/mol is condition-specific, not universal.

Learning Objective: Apply proportional reasoning, Kelvin conversion, and controlled-variable recognition.
Easy

Trend

A gas is compressed at constant T and n. What happens to P?

P increases. Boyle’s law is inverse.
Medium

Kelvin calculation

P₂ = 1.20 atm × 363.15 K288.15 K = 1.51 atm
Hard

Choose the law

A flexible balloon warms while external pressure stays constant. Which law applies?

Charles’s law: V and T change.
