What Can Happen When a Solute Enters Water?

Learning Objective: Explain that a solute in water may form ions or remain molecular, and connect that outcome to the electrolyte concept.

Path 1 — Ions form

solute + H₂O → ions in solution

This can happen by dissociation (ionic compounds) or ionization (some molecular substances).

Path 2 — No ions form

solute + H₂O → molecules only

The substance dissolves, but the particles remain neutral molecules.

Dissociation

NaCl(s)→Na⁺Cl⁻

pre-existing ions separate

Ionization

CH₃COOH⇌H⁺CH₃COO⁻

molecules form ions in water

No ion formation

C₆H₁₂O₆→C₆H₁₂O₆(aq)

molecules remain intact

NaCl(s) → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq)
C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) remains molecular
Electrolyte link: if ions are present in water, the solution is an electrolyte. If no ions are present, it is a nonelectrolyte.
Next step: once ions form, the amount of ion formation determines whether the electrolyte is strong or weak.

From Formula Units to Ions

Formula Unit

one neutral ionic formula unit

In Water

Li⁺Li⁺SO₄²⁻

Main Idea

subscripts in the formula become coefficients of the ions after dissociation

Li₂SO₄(aq) → 2Li⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq)
Formula-unit thinking: the subscript 2 in Li₂SO₄ becomes the coefficient 2 in front of Li⁺ after dissociation.

Mole Ratio

1 mol Li₂SO₄ → 2 mol Li⁺ + 1 mol SO₄²⁻

Careful!

total ions and requested ion are not always the same thing

Guided Calculation — Ions from Moles

Learning Objective: Calculate moles and number of ions produced by a soluble ionic compound.
Guided Calculation
How many Li⁺ ions are present when 2.40 mol Li₂SO₄ fully dissociates in water?
Path: mol Li₂SO₄ → mol Li⁺ → Li⁺ ions
2.40 mol Li₂SO₄ × 2 mol Li⁺1 mol Li₂SO₄
mol Li₂SO₄ cancels; mol Li⁺ remains
2.40 mol Li₂SO₄ → 4.80 mol Li⁺
4.80 mol Li⁺ × 6.022×10²³ Li⁺ ions1 mol Li⁺
Number of Li⁺ ions = [ _______ ]
Reasonableness: more than 1 mole of formula units and 2 Li⁺ per unit should give more than 10²⁴ ions.

ACS-Style Electrolyte Ranking Practice

Learning Objective: Solve common electrolyte-conductivity ranking problems using solubility, acid/base strength, and number of ions formed.

Problem Type 1 — Soluble? Strong? How Many Ions?

Question: At the same concentration, which solution would conduct electricity most strongly?

A. NaCl(aq)
B. CaCl₂(aq)
C. AgCl in water
(given: AgCl is insoluble)
D. C₆H₁₂O₆(aq)
  • Step 1: eliminate the nonelectrolyte and the insoluble compound.
  • Step 2: among strong electrolytes, compare how many ions each formula unit produces.
Reasoning: AgCl is explicitly given as insoluble here, and glucose is a nonelectrolyte. Both NaCl and CaCl₂ are soluble strong electrolytes, but NaCl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻ gives 2 ions, while CaCl₂ → Ca²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ gives 3 ions.
Answer: CaCl₂(aq) conducts most strongly.
ACS pattern: for strong electrolytes at equal concentration, more dissolved ions usually means stronger conductivity.
T4.2 transition: In this problem, AgCl is labeled insoluble for you. In the next Topic, we will learn how to decide soluble vs. insoluble using the solubility chart.

Problem Type 2 — Strong vs. Weak Electrolyte Conductivity

Question: At the same analytical concentration, which solutions belong in the stronger-conductivity group and which belong in the weaker-conductivity group?

HCl(aq)
NaOH(aq)
CH₃COOH(aq)
NH₃(aq)
Reasoning: HCl is a strong acid and NaOH is a strong base, so both are strong electrolytes. CH₃COOH is a weak acid, and NH₃ is a weak base, so both are weak electrolytes.
Category-level conclusion: HCl and NaOH generally conduct much more strongly than CH₃COOH and NH₃ at the same analytical concentration. Exact conductivity within each pair can differ because conductivity also depends on ion mobility and, for weak electrolytes, the extent of ionization.
Fast decision rule: first classify each substance as strong electrolyte, weak electrolyte, or nonelectrolyte; use exact within-category ranking only when enough additional information is provided.
