Enthalpy — First Build the Picture, Then the Definition

Learning Objective: Develop an intuitive picture of enthalpy and then state its chemical definition, H = E + PV.
Intuitive situation: imagine a reaction occurring in an open beaker. The reaction system has its own internal energy, but if gases expand or the system volume changes, it can also push against the surrounding atmosphere.

Inside the system

E

internal energy of the particles in the system

The system also occupies space

surrounding pressure acts on that volume

pressure × volume gives the PV term

Chemical definition: the enthalpy, H, of a system is defined as the sum of its internal energy and the pressure–volume term.
H = E + PV
Einternal energy
+
PVpressure × volume term
Student-friendly meaning: enthalpy is a convenient energy bookkeeping property for a system when pressure–volume effects matter. In chemistry, it becomes especially useful because many reactions are studied at approximately constant pressure.
Do not say “enthalpy is heat.” H is a property of the system. Heat, q, is energy being transferred. Only under the constant-pressure condition do we get the useful relationship qp = ΔH.

Why Does Constant-Pressure Heat Equal ΔH?

Learning Objective: Connect ΔE = q + w and H = E + PV to the constant-pressure result qₚ = ΔH.
Start from what we already know:
ΔE = q + w
for pressure–volume work: w = −PΔV
so at constant pressure: ΔE = qp − PΔV
Now look at the change in enthalpy:
H = E + PV
at constant P: ΔH = ΔE + PΔV
ΔH = (qp − PΔV) + PΔV
The PΔV terms cancel.
ΔH = qp
Why this is useful: for a reaction at constant pressure, the heat transferred by the reaction system directly tells us the reaction's enthalpy change.
coffee-cup ΔT→ qsurroundings→ qreaction = −qsurroundings→ ΔHreaction
Condition lock: this simple equality assumes constant pressure and that pressure–volume work is the only work mode being considered.

Exothermic vs. Endothermic — Use the Temperature Change to Read the Reaction

Learning Objective: Use the measured temperature change of the solution to determine q for the surroundings, then infer q and ΔH for the reaction system.

EXOTHERMIC — ΔHreaction < 0

1. What we measure
Tfinal, solution > Tinitial, solution
→ ΔTsolution > 0
2. Reaction system
The solution got warmer because the reaction lost heat.
→ qreaction < 0
3. Heat direction
reaction → solution / surroundings
4. Words
reaction releases / produces / gives off heat
5. Constant P
qp,reaction = ΔHreaction
→ ΔHreaction < 0

ENDOTHERMIC — ΔHreaction > 0

1. What we measure
Tfinal, solution < Tinitial, solution
→ ΔTsolution < 0
2. Reaction system
The solution got cooler because the reaction gained heat.
→ qreaction > 0
3. Heat direction
solution / surroundings → reaction
4. Words
reaction absorbs / requires heat
5. Constant P
qp,reaction = ΔHreaction
→ ΔHreaction > 0
System lock: the reaction is the system. We measure the solution temperature, then infer the reaction sign.

Exothermic chain

ΔTsolution > 0→ qreaction < 0→ ΔHreaction < 0

Endothermic chain

ΔTsolution < 0→ qreaction > 0→ ΔHreaction > 0
Important: ΔT belongs to the solution we measure. The reaction sign is determined from heat direction, not from a separate ΔTreaction.

Why is ΔHreaction negative?

Reactants: higher H
Products: lower H

The reaction system moves to a lower enthalpy state.
At constant pressure, the enthalpy difference leaves the system as heat.

Why is ΔHreaction positive?

Reactants: lower H
Products: higher H

The reaction system moves to a higher enthalpy state.
At constant pressure, energy must enter the system as heat.

What we measure: ΔTsolution  →  What we want: qreaction and ΔHreaction

Worked Example — Butane Combustion: ΔH vs. ΔE

Learning Objective: Use constant-pressure heat and work signs to distinguish reaction enthalpy from internal-energy change.
Problem: When 1 mol of butane burns at constant pressure, the reaction produces 2658 kJ of heat and does 3 kJ of work on the surroundings. Find ΔH and ΔE.
1. Systembutane combustion reaction
2. “produces heat”q = −2658 kJ
3. “work on surroundings”w = −3 kJ
4. Constant Pqp = ΔH
ΔH = qp = −2658 kJ
Negative ΔH → exothermic combustion
ΔE = q + w
ΔE = (−2658 kJ) + (−3 kJ) = −2661 kJ
Reasonableness: the system loses energy both as heat and as work, so ΔE must be more negative than q alone.
Do not force ΔH = ΔE. They are close here because the work term is small, but they represent different state-function changes.

Coffee-Cup Reaction — Temperature Rise → Reaction Enthalpy

Learning Objective: Translate a measured surroundings temperature change into q for the reaction and then into ΔH at constant pressure.
Problem: 0.158 g Mg reacts with excess HCl in a coffee-cup calorimeter containing 100.0 mL of solution. The solution warms from 25.6 °C to 32.8 °C. Use density = 1.00 g/mL and csolution = 4.18 J/(g·°C).

Calculate ΔHreaction in kJ per mol Mg reacted.
solution warms→ ΔTsurr > 0→ qsurr > 0→ qrxn < 0
ΔTsolution = 32.8 − 25.6 = +7.2 °C
qsolution = mcΔT = (100.0 g)(4.18 J/(g·°C))(+7.2 °C) = +3.01 kJ
qrxn = −qsolution = −3.0 kJ
At constant pressure: ΔHrxn = qp,rxn = −3.0 kJ
But −3.0 kJ is for the amount of Mg actually used: 0.158 g Mg. To report the enthalpy change per 1 mol Mg reacting, first convert the Mg sample to moles, then scale the heat to 1 mol.
1. Convert Mg to moles 0.158 g Mg × 1 mol Mg 24.305 g Mg = 0.00650 mol Mg
2. What we know 0.00650 mol Mg → −3.0 kJ
3. Scale to 1 mol Mg −3.0 kJ × 1 mol Mg 0.00650 mol Mg
4. Report per mole −4.6 × 10² kJ/mol Mg
Meaning: if 1 mol Mg reacts under these conditions, about 463 kJ of heat is released.
Interpretation: the solution warmed because the reaction released heat. Therefore the reaction is exothermic and ΔH is negative.
Preview: in the next topic, ΔH will be attached directly to a balanced chemical equation and used as a stoichiometric heat ratio.

ACS-Style Practice — Read the Observation, Then Assign the Reaction Sign

Learning Objective: Use observations and wording to distinguish surroundings temperature change from reaction heat and enthalpy signs.
ACS-style

Question A — Warm solution

A reaction occurs in a coffee-cup calorimeter. The solution temperature increases from 22.0 °C to 29.5 °C. Which statement is correct?

A. ΔTsolution < 0 and ΔHrxn < 0
B. ΔTsolution > 0 and ΔHrxn < 0
C. ΔTsolution > 0 and ΔHrxn > 0
D. ΔTsolution = 0 and ΔHrxn = 0
ACS-style

Question B — Language trap

At constant pressure, a reaction absorbs 42 kJ of heat while doing 5 kJ of work on the surroundings. Which pair is correct?

A. ΔH = −42 kJ; w = −5 kJ
B. ΔH = +42 kJ; w = +5 kJ
C. ΔH = +42 kJ; w = −5 kJ
D. ΔH = −42 kJ; w = +5 kJ
Read words separately: “absorbs heat” controls q; “work on surroundings” controls w.
A: B. The surroundings warm, so qsurr > 0 and qrxn = ΔHrxn < 0.
B: C. At constant pressure, qp = ΔH = +42 kJ. Work done by the system on surroundings gives w = −5 kJ.
Fast exam chain: observation/word → whose quantity? → direction → sign → constant-P relation.
