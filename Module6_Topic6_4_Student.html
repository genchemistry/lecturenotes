From ΔH to a Chemical Equation — Heat Becomes a Stoichiometric Quantity

Learning Objective: Connect T6.3 enthalpy signs to thermochemical equations and interpret ΔH as the heat associated with the balanced reaction as written.
Transition from T6.3: We learned that at constant pressure, qp = ΔH. Now we attach that ΔH directly to a balanced chemical equation.
CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l)    ΔH = −890.4 kJ
A balanced equation + physical states + ΔH = a thermochemical equation.

Balanced coefficients

tell how many moles react or form

Physical states

(s), (l), (g), or (aq) matter

ΔH

belongs to the reaction as written

Read it in words: when 1 mol CH₄ reacts with 2 mol O₂ to form 1 mol CO₂ and 2 mol H₂O(l), the reaction releases 890.4 kJ.
CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l) + 890.4 kJ
Exothermic: heat is released, so we can picture heat on the product side.   ΔH = −890.4 kJ
What if the process is reversed? The reverse process must absorb the same amount of heat.
CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l) + 890.4 kJ → CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g)
Endothermic: heat is required, so we can picture heat on the reactant side.   ΔH = +890.4 kJ

Read the Thermochemical Equation — State, Sign, Exothermic or Endothermic?

Learning Objective: Use physical state, ΔH sign, and heat placement to classify thermochemical equations from simple to more challenging cases.
1. Easy — Sign only
A reaction has ΔH = −125 kJ. Is it exothermic or endothermic?
Exothermic — ΔH is negative.
2. Easy — Heat placement
A + B → C + heat. Is the reaction exothermic or endothermic?
Exothermic — heat appears on the product side.
3. Medium — State change
H₂O(s) → H₂O(l)    ΔH = +6.01 kJ
Is the process exothermic or endothermic? What physical change occurs?
Endothermic — melting; heat must be absorbed.
4. Medium — Reverse the process
H₂O(l) → H₂O(s)    ΔH = ?
ΔH = −6.01 kJ — freezing is the reverse of melting, so the sign changes.
5. Harder — Same formula, different physical state
Compare:

H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(l)    ΔH = −285.8 kJ
H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → H₂O(g)    ΔH = −241.8 kJ

Which reaction releases more heat? Why can the ΔH values differ even though both form H₂O?
Forming H₂O(l) releases more heat. Physical state is part of the thermochemical equation; liquid water is at lower enthalpy than water vapor.
Rule: never ignore the state symbols. H₂O(l) and H₂O(g) do not represent the same enthalpy state.

Heat Stoichiometry — Treat ΔH Like a Mole Conversion Ratio

Learning Objective: Build conversion factors between moles of reaction substances and heat from a thermochemical equation.
CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l)   ΔH = −890.4 kJ
given amount→ mol substance→ kJ using ΔH ratio
From CH₄ to heat−890.4 kJ1 mol CH₄
From O₂ to heat−890.4 kJ2 mol O₂
From heat to CH₄1 mol CH₄−890.4 kJ
Do not use ΔH as a free-standing number. Its mole meaning comes from the coefficients in the balanced equation.

Worked Problem — Mass of Fuel → Heat Released

Learning Objective: Calculate reaction heat from a given mass using molar mass and the thermochemical ΔH ratio.
Problem: A tank contains 13.2 kg C₃H₈. For the reaction below, calculate the heat associated with complete combustion of all the propane.
C₃H₈(g) + 5 O₂(g) → 3 CO₂(g) + 4 H₂O(g)   ΔH = −2043 kJ
Given: molar mass C₃H₈ = 44.09 g/mol
13.2 kg C₃H₈→ g C₃H₈→ mol C₃H₈→ kJ
13.2 kg C₃H₈× 1000 g C₃H₈1 kg C₃H₈× 1 mol C₃H₈44.09 g C₃H₈× −2043 kJ1 mol C₃H₈
q = −6.12 × 10⁵ kJ
Interpretation: negative q means the reaction system releases heat. The same result can be stated as 6.12 × 10⁵ kJ released.
Reasonableness: about 300 mol propane × about 2000 kJ/mol ≈ 6 × 10⁵ kJ.

Worked Problem — Heat Required → Mass of Fuel

Learning Objective: Use a thermochemical equation in reverse as a conversion pathway from heat to moles and grams.
Problem: Butane combustion releases 2658 kJ for every 1 mol C₄H₁₀ burned. What mass of butane is needed to release 1.50 × 10³ kJ?
1.50 × 10³ kJ released→ mol C₄H₁₀→ g C₄H₁₀
Magnitude first: use 1500 kJ as the amount of heat released. The negative sign describes direction; the amount requested is positive.
1.50 × 10³ kJ× 1 mol C₄H₁₀2658 kJ× 58.12 g C₄H₁₀1 mol C₄H₁₀
mass C₄H₁₀ = 32.8 g
Reasonableness: 1500 kJ is a little more than half of 2658 kJ, so we need a little more than half a mole of butane. Half a mole is about 29 g, so 32.8 g is reasonable.
Integrated Slide 49 · Local Slide 6

Heat Stoichiometry Can Continue — Heat → Reactant → Product

Learning Objective: Combine a ΔH conversion with ordinary mole-ratio stoichiometry to determine the amount of product formed.
Continue the previous problem: If enough butane burns to release 1.50 × 10³ kJ, what mass of CO₂ is produced?
2 C₄H₁₀(g) + 13 O₂(g) → 8 CO₂(g) + 10 H₂O
kJ→mol C₄H₁₀→mol CO₂→g CO₂
1.50 × 10³ kJ× 1 mol C₄H₁₀2658 kJ× 4 mol CO₂1 mol C₄H₁₀× 44.01 g CO₂1 mol CO₂
mass CO₂ = 99.3 g
ΔH gives the heat ↔ mole bridge; the balanced equation supplies the ordinary mole ratio.

ACS-Style Practice — Thermochemical Equation Traps

Learning Objective: Use coefficient, direction, state, and sign reasoning to eliminate common thermochemical-equation distractors.
ACS-style

Question A

H₂O(s) → H₂O(l)   ΔH = +6.01 kJ. What is ΔH for 3 H₂O(s) → 3 H₂O(l)?

A. +2.00 kJ
B. +6.01 kJ
C. +18.03 kJ
D. −18.03 kJ
ACS-style

Question B

For CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l), ΔH = −890.4 kJ. Which statement is correct?

A. Burning 2 mol CH₄ releases 890.4 kJ.
B. Forming 1 mol H₂O(l) releases 890.4 kJ.
C. Burning 0.50 mol CH₄ releases 445.2 kJ.
D. Reversing the reaction keeps ΔH = −890.4 kJ.
A: C. Multiply the entire equation by 3 → multiply ΔH by 3.
B: C. 0.50 mol CH₄ corresponds to half the reaction amount, so half of 890.4 kJ is released.
Fast check: coefficients → reaction direction → physical states → sign and magnitude of ΔH.
