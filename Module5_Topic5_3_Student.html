Worked Ideal Gas Law — Find Volume

Learning Objective: Solve PV = nRT for volume using a N₂ problem and verify units and magnitude.
Given: n = 0.845 mol N₂, P = 1.37 atm, T = 315 K. Find V.
PV = nRT→solve for V→substitute matched units
V = nRTP
V = (0.845 mol)(0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(315 K)1.37 atm
Unit check: mol, K, and atm cancel, leaving L.
V = 15.9 L
Reasonableness: about 0.85 mol near room temperature and modest pressure should occupy on the order of tens of liters, so ~16 L is reasonable.

Worked Ideal Gas Law — Find Moles with Large-Unit Conversions

Learning Objective: Convert volume, pressure, and temperature before solving the ideal gas law for moles.
Worked problem: How many moles of N₂ fill a room of 1232 ft³ at 878 mmHg and 23.0 °C?
ft³ → L→mmHg → atm→°C → K→n = PV/RT
1232 ft³ × (12 in)³(1 ft)³ × (2.54 cm)³(1 in)³ × 1 L1000 cm³ = 34,886 L
878 mmHg × 1 atm760 mmHg = 1.155 atm     T = 23.0 + 273.15 = 296.15 K
n = PVRT
n = (1.155 atm)(34,886 L)(0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(296.15 K) = 1.66 × 103 mol N₂
Reasonableness: a room contains a very large gas volume, so a result on the order of 10³ mol is plausible.

STP and the Ideal Gas Law — Shortcut or General Equation?

Learning Objective: Use the course STP convention correctly and distinguish the STP molar-volume shortcut from the general ideal gas law.
Course STP: 273.15 K and 1 atm
At these conditions only: 1 mol ideal gas ≈ 22.4 L

At STP

If the problem is explicitly at 273.15 K and 1 atm, the molar-volume relationship can be used.

1 mol ↔ 22.4 L

Not at STP

Use the actual P and T in the ideal gas law.

PV = nRT
Do not treat 22.4 L/mol as universal. Gas volume changes when pressure or temperature changes.

Gas Density from the Ideal Gas Law

Learning Objective: Derive the gas-density equation from familiar definitions and predict how density changes with P, M, and T.

Density

d = mV

mass per volume, usually g/L

Moles from Mass

n = mM

M = molar mass in g/mol

Start:   PV = nRT
Replace n with mM:   PV = mRTM
Divide by V:   P = mVRTM
d = PMRT
P ↑ → d ↑

more gas packed into a given volume

M ↑ → d ↑

heavier particles at the same P and T

T ↑ → d ↓

gas expands at fixed P

Worked Gas Density — CO₂ at Two Conditions

Learning Objective: Calculate gas density from pressure, temperature, and molar mass and interpret the direction of change.
Worked comparison: Find the density of CO₂ at STP and at 65 °C and 1.13 atm. M(CO₂) = 44.01 g/mol.
d = PMRT

At STP

d = (1.00 atm)(44.01 g·mol⁻¹)(0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(273.15 K)
d = 1.963 g/L

65 °C and 1.13 atm

T = 65 + 273.15 = 338.15 K

d = (1.13 atm)(44.01 g·mol⁻¹)(0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(338.15 K)
d = 1.792 g/L
Interpretation: the higher pressure tends to increase density, while the much higher temperature tends to decrease it. Here the temperature effect is large enough that the second density is lower than at STP.

Molar Mass from Gas Data — Route Selection + Worked Identification

Learning Objectives: Select an efficient molar-mass equation from density data or from measured mass and PVT data. Calculate molar mass from gas density and use the result to identify a plausible gas.

Route A — Density is given

Start from d = PM/RT and solve for M.

M = dRTP

Route B — Mass and volume are given

Use n = m/M inside PV=nRT, then solve for M.

M = mRTPV
Unit target: molar mass must finish in g/mol. If the result does not, inspect the R units and the pressure/volume conversions.
Constructed identification problem: An unknown gas has d = 1.80 g/L at 1.00 atm and 25.0 °C. Which is most plausible: He, N₂, O₂, or CO₂?
density + P + T→M = dRT/P→compare molar masses
T = 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.15 K
M = (1.80 g·L⁻¹)(0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)(298.15 K)1.00 atm
M ≈ 44.0 g/mol → closest choice: CO₂ (44.01 g/mol)
Check: density is not the identity by itself; density first gives molar mass under the stated P and T.

Wrong R

Pressure units do not match the units built into R.

°C instead of K

Creates physically meaningless gas-law ratios and results.
