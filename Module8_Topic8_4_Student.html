Ionization Energy — What Is Being Measured?

Learning Objective: Define ionization energy and interpret what a larger ionization energy means.
Ionization Energy (IE) = energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion.
M(g) + energy → M⁺(g) + e⁻

Energy sign

Electron removal requires energy, so ionization is endothermic for the reaction as written.

Meaning of a large IE

The electron is held more tightly and is harder to remove.

Ionization Energy is added to the integrated map: generally increases up and right.

First Ionization Energy — Trend and Cause

Learning Objective: Explain the first-ionization-energy trend using Zeff and electron distance, then apply it to a comparison.

Across a period

Zeff increases and radius generally decreases → electrons are held more tightly → IE increases.

Down a group

More occupied shells and greater distance from the nucleus → outer electrons are easier to remove → IE decreases.

Quick check

Compare K, Ca, Rb.

Ca is to the right of K in the same period; Rb is below K.

Ca > K > Rb in first ionization energy
Stronger attraction + shorter distance → harder electron removal → higher IE

Ionization-Energy Exceptions — Configuration Matters

Learning Objective: Explain common first-ionization-energy exceptions using subshell energy and electron pairing.

The trend is general, not perfectly smooth

The highlighted pairs show two common exceptions:

Be vs B and N vs O

Use the block colors to see which subshells are involved.

Be vs B

B begins the 2p subshell. A 2p electron is easier to remove than Be's filled 2s electron.

IE₁(B) < IE₁(Be)

N vs O

O has one paired 2p orbital, adding electron–electron repulsion, so one electron is easier to remove.

IE₁(O) < IE₁(N)

How to think: trend first, then ask whether a change in subshell energy or pairing repulsion explains the exception.

Electron Affinity — Energy Change for Electron Addition

Learning Objective: Define electron affinity and interpret negative, positive, and near-zero electron-affinity values.
Electron Affinity (EA) = energy change when an electron is added to a gaseous atom or ion.
X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g)

Negative EA

Energy is released when the electron is added.

Cl: EA = −346 kJ/mol

Near zero / positive

Electron addition may be neutral or require energy.

N ≈ 0; Be ≥ 0

Critical sign rule: EA is not always negative. It can be negative, positive, or approximately zero.

Electron Affinity — Broad Periodic Pattern

Learning Objective: Describe the broad electron-affinity pattern while recognizing that electron affinity has important irregularities.
Broad pattern: for many main-group elements, electron affinity becomes more negative across a period and often less negative down a group.
But: EA is much less regular than atomic radius or ionization energy.
Dashed purple EA arrows = broad pattern only, not an exception-free rule.

IE vs EA — Different Questions

Learning Objective: Distinguish ionization energy from electron affinity and connect both to broad chemical behavior.

Ionization Energy

M(g) → M⁺(g) + e⁻

Question: How hard is it to remove an electron?

The required energy is the ionization energy.

Electron Affinity

X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g)

Question: What energy change occurs when an electron is added?

The sign tells whether energy is released or required.

Reactive metals

Low IE; electron loss is relatively easy.

Reactive nonmetals

High IE and often highly negative EA; they strongly retain electrons and often favor electron gain.

Noble gases

Very high IE and unfavorable EA; they tend to neither lose nor gain electrons.

IE = removal question    |    EA = addition question

Metallic Character — Connect It to Ionization Energy

Learning Objective: Define metallic character, connect it to electron loss, and predict its periodic trend.
Metallic character describes how strongly an element shows typical metallic behavior.

Electron behavior

Metals tend to lose valence electrons and form cations.

Connection to ionization energy

Lower ionization energy means an electron is easier to remove.

Lower IE → easier electron loss → greater metallic character.

Periodic trend: metallic character generally increases down and to the left.
Metallic Character is the newest trend: vertical arrow added on the far left, horizontal arrow added at the bottom.
