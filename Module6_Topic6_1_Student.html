Energy, Heat, and Work — Conservation of Energy in Thermochemistry

Learning Objective: Distinguish energy, work, heat, and temperature, and connect these ideas to conservation of energy and the First Law of Thermodynamics.
Law of conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only be transferred or transformed.
✦
moving ball → collision → energy transferred → second ball moves
First Law of Thermodynamics: this conservation idea applied to thermochemistry. Energy can move between a system and its surroundings as heat or work.

Work

energy transfer when a force causes motion

Heat

energy transfer because of a temperature difference

Warmer object

heat →

Cooler object

Do not use heat and temperature as synonyms. Temperature describes the thermal state of a sample; heat describes energy in transfer because of a temperature difference.

First Law of Thermodynamics — Tracking Energy for the System

Learning Objective: Use the First Law of Thermodynamics to track how the energy of a defined system changes relative to its surroundings.
System first: thermochemistry tracks the energy of a chosen system. The First Law of Thermodynamics says energy is conserved as it is transferred between the system and surroundings.
ΔEsystem = −ΔEsurroundings
Energy gained by one part of the universe must be lost by another part.
Universe accounting: ΔEuniverse = ΔEsystem + ΔEsurroundings = 0.

Internal Energy of the System — A State Function

Learning Objective: Define the internal energy of a system and explain why ΔE depends only on the initial and final states.
Internal energy, E: the total microscopic kinetic and potential energy of all particles in the system.
ΔE = Efinal − Einitial
Different paths can connect the same two states, but ΔE is the same.
State function: a property determined by the state of the system, not by the path used to reach that state.

Learning Objective: Calculate the change in the system's internal energy by combining signed heat and work transfers.
Internal energy is the target: q and w are the two transfer terms that determine ΔE.
Example: A cylinder and piston assembly is the system. It absorbs 559 J of heat and expands, doing 488 J of work on the surroundings.
1. Define systemcylinder + contents
2. Heat directioninto system → q = +559 J
3. Work directionby system → w = −488 J
4. PredictΔE should be small and positive
ΔE = q + w
ΔE = (+559 J) + (−488 J)
ΔE = +71 J
Reasonableness: the system absorbs slightly more energy as heat than it transfers out as work, so its internal energy increases slightly.

Work as Energy Transfer — Magnitude and Path Dependence

Learning Objective: Relate mechanical work to force and displacement, and distinguish work from state functions such as internal energy.
General work idea: work is energy transferred when a force acts through a distance.
mechanical work magnitude = force × displacement

This gives the physical meaning behind the thermodynamic work term, w.

State function

ΔE depends only on the initial and final states.

Path-dependent transfer

q and w depend on how the process occurs.

Important: work is not a state function. Two different paths between the same initial and final states can involve different amounts of work.
Next layer: for gases in General Chemistry I, the most useful work model is pressure–volume work.
general work idea → pressure–volume work: w = −PextΔV

Focus on the Work Term, w — Pressure–Volume Work

Learning Objective: Focus on the work term in ΔE = q + w and connect expansion and compression to the sign of pressure–volume work.
Now isolate one term: internal energy can change through heat or work. In this topic, we now focus on work, w. The heat term, q, will be developed next through calorimetry.
w = −PextΔV

Expansion

ΔV > 0 → w < 0

work done by system

Compression

ΔV < 0 → w > 0

work done on system

Why the minus sign? During expansion, the system transfers energy to the surroundings by pushing against the external pressure. That energy leaves the system, so w is negative.

Example: A gas expands from 2.00 L to 5.00 L against a constant external pressure of 1.20 atm.
1. Directionexpansion
2. ΔV5.00 − 2.00 = +3.00 L
3. Predict signw must be negative
4. PathL·atm → J
w = −PextΔV = −(1.20 atm)(+3.00 L) = −3.60 L·atm
−3.60 L·atm × 101.325 J1 L·atm

ACS-Style Practice 1 — System Signs and Internal Energy

Learning Objective: Use the system perspective to eliminate sign-convention distractors before calculating ΔE.
Exam strategy: do not start with arithmetic. First identify the system, then the direction of heat and work, then assign signs.
1Define system
2Heat direction
3Work direction
4Apply ΔE = q + w
ACS-style

Question A — Sign logic

A gas is the system. It releases 325 J of heat while 140 J of work is done on the gas. Which pair of values is correct?

A. q = +325 J, w = +140 J
B. q = −325 J, w = +140 J
C. q = +325 J, w = −140 J
D. q = −325 J, w = −140 J
ACS-style

Question B — Predict before calculating

For the same system, which statement about ΔE is correct?

A. ΔE must be positive because work is done on the system.
B. ΔE must be negative because more energy leaves as heat than enters as work.
C. ΔE = 0 because heat and work always cancel.
D. The sign cannot be known from the information given.
Fast check: compare magnitudes after signs are assigned: 325 J leaves, 140 J enters → net energy leaves the system.
A: B. Heat leaves the system → q = −325 J. Work done on the system → w = +140 J.
ΔE = (−325 J) + (+140 J) = −185 J
B: B. The system loses more energy as heat than it gains as work, so internal energy decreases.
Distractor warning: words such as “releases,” “on,” and “by” are sign information. Read them before touching the calculator.

ACS-Style Practice 2 — Pressure–Volume Work and Distractor Checks

Learning Objective: Use expansion/compression, ΔV, units, and sign reasoning to solve pressure–volume work problems efficiently.
ACS-style

Question A — Expansion work

A gas expands from 3.00 L to 7.00 L against a constant external pressure of 1.50 atm. Which setup is correct?

A. w = +(1.50 atm)(4.00 L)
B. w = −(1.50 atm)(4.00 L)
C. w = −(1.50 atm)(10.00 L)
D. w = +(1.50 atm)(10.00 L)
Expansion → ΔV > 0 → work done by system → w < 0
ACS-style

Question B — Compression + unit reasoning

A gas is compressed from 5.20 L to 2.20 L against 0.800 atm. Which statement must be true?

A. ΔV = +3.00 L and w < 0
B. ΔV = −3.00 L and w < 0
C. ΔV = −3.00 L and w > 0
D. ΔV = +3.00 L and w > 0

Trap 1

Using Vinitial − Vfinal instead of ΔV = Vfinal − Vinitial.

Trap 2

Forgetting the minus sign in w = −PextΔV.

Trap 3

Reporting L·atm when the requested answer is in joules.

A: B. ΔV = 7.00 − 3.00 = +4.00 L, so w = −(1.50)(4.00) = −6.00 L·atm ≈ −608 J.
B: C. ΔV = 2.20 − 5.20 = −3.00 L, so compression gives w > 0.
ACS fast check: direction → sign of ΔV → sign of w → unit conversion → magnitude. These checks can eliminate plausible distractors before full arithmetic.
