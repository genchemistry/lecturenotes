Draw Lewis Structures: Dots → Pairs → Lines

Learning Objective: Build a Lewis structure from the central atom and its valence-electron dots.
1. Choose the central atomFor H₂O, O goes in the center. H can make only one bond, so both H atoms are terminal.
2. Count total valence electronsValence electrons (VE): H contributes 1 each; O contributes 6. Total VE = 8. Add for negative charge; subtract for positive charge in ions.
3. Place the central-atom dotsO is in group 6A. Draw six dots: one on each side first, then pair the remaining two.
4. Add the terminal-atom dotsEach H contributes one dot. All eight valence electrons are now visible.
5. Group the future shared pairsAn oval joins the O dot and H dot that will make one bond. Keep all dot positions fixed.
6. Two shared dots become one lineRemove the ovals and add two O–H lines. Fade only the four dots represented by those lines; keep both oxygen lone pairs clear.
7. Complete shells and place remaining electronsH has a duet; O has an octet and two lone pairs. Total: 4 bonding + 4 nonbonding = 8 e⁻. This straight drawing is connectivity, not the 3D shape.
8. If needed, make a multiple bond; then checkIn other structures, use a neighboring lone pair if a second-period center lacks an octet. Four shared dots make a double bond. Check total VE, shells, and formal charges.

Worked Example: CO₂ Needs Multiple Bonds

Learning Objective: Convert lone pairs into bonds without adding electrons.
Total VE: 4 + 2(6) = 16 e⁻
Start O–C–O. The two bonds use 4 e⁻. Completing the terminal oxygen octets uses the remaining 12 e⁻; carbon still has only 4 surrounding electrons.
Move one lone pair from each oxygen into a C–O bond. No electrons are added: the two single bonds become two double bonds.
Final count: 8 bonding + 8 nonbonding = 16 e⁻. Every atom has an octet. A double bond is one connected region for the geometry analysis that follows.

Build H₂CO: Dots → Shared Pairs → Lines

Learning Objective: Draw the Lewis structure one electron-accounting step at a time.
1. Count total VEH₂CO: 2(1) + 4 + 6 = 12 e⁻.
2. Choose the centerC is less electronegative than O and can make several bonds. Put C in the center; O, H, H around it.
3. Start with carbon dotsC is in group 4A. Put four single dots around C.
4. Add oxygen dotsO is in group 6A. Add six dots: two lone pairs and two single dots.
5. Add hydrogen dotsEach H contributes one dot. All 12 valence electrons are now shown.
6. Identify the shared pairsEach oval groups the two dots that will form one bond. Keep every dot in its original position; do not move the pair together yet.
7. Two dots → one lineRemove the three ovals and draw one line for each shared pair. The six bonding dots stay in place at low opacity; unpaired dots and lone pairs remain clear.
8. Make another shared C–O pairThe remaining single dots on C and O form a second shared pair between the same atoms.
9. Four shared dots → two linesThe two C–O shared pairs are a double bond. Final: 8 bonding e⁻ + 4 nonbonding e⁻ = 12 e⁻; C and O have octets, H has a duet.

Formal Charge: Count Electrons Assigned to One Atom

Learning Objective: Apply the formal-charge formula to a target atom.
FC (for a target atom) = VE of target atom − nonbonding e⁻ − number of bonds
Count individual bond lines: a single bond counts as 1, a double bond as 2, and a triple bond as 3. Use nonbonding electrons, not the number of lone pairs.
Target atom: O in H₂OCount
VE of target atom6
Nonbonding electrons4
Number of bonds (individual lines)2
Formal charge6 − 4 − 2 = 0
For an octet, count both electrons in every shared pair. For formal charge, assign one electron from each bond line to the target atom. All formal charges must add to the total charge of the molecule or ion.

Both atoms have octets; the formal charges add to zero. This is a valid example where the negative formal charge is on the less electronegative atom.
Choose valid electron counts and second-period octets first. Then usually favor smaller formal-charge magnitudes and, among otherwise comparable structures, negative charge on the more electronegative atom. Formal charge is different from bond polarity.

Resonance: Ozone Has One Electronic Structure

Learning Objective: Keep the atoms fixed while redistributing electrons.
Formal charges help us evaluate a drawing. What if more than one valid drawing keeps the same atom connections? That brings us to resonance.
O₃ total VE: 3(6) = 18 e⁻
↔
Same nuclei and connectivity · two resonance contributors
The nuclei and connectivity stay fixed. Only the locations of bonding and nonbonding electrons change. Both drawings use 18 electrons and have a total formal charge of zero.
The real molecule is a resonance hybrid, not a molecule switching between drawings. The two O–O bonds are equivalent; the simple average bond order is 1.5. Delocalization stabilizes the electronic structure relative to a localized description.
Integrated Slide 37 · Local Slide 11

Worked Example: Nitrate, NO₃⁻

Learning Objective: Relate equivalent resonance contributors to equal bond lengths.
Total VE: 5 + 3(6) + 1 = 24 e⁻
One N=O and two N–O bonds complete the nitrogen octet. N has +1 formal charge; each singly bonded O has −1; the double-bonded O has 0. Sum: +1 −1 −1 = −1.
↔↔
These three equivalent contributors differ only in the position of the N=O bond and electron pairs. The three N–O bonds are equivalent in the hybrid; average bond order = (2 + 1 + 1)/3 = 4/3.

Resonance or Different Connectivity (Isomer)?

Learning Objective: Distinguish resonance contributors, structural isomers, and cis–trans isomers.
ResonanceSame nuclei, same connectionsDifferent electron placementOne delocalized electronic structure
Structural isomersSame molecular formulaDifferent connectionsDifferent compounds
Cis–trans isomersSame formula and connectionsDifferent fixed spatial arrangementDifferent compounds
Recall resonance: the two ozone contributors on the previous slide describe one molecule; only the electron placement changes.
Structural isomers: different attachments around O create different compounds; we will draw the full C₃H₈O pair next.
Cis–trans isomers: the connections remain fixed, but the relative arrangement across C=C differs. The complete 2-butene comparison follows.

Same Formula, Different Connections: Structural Isomers

Learning Objective: Compare two valid structures with the formula C₃H₈O.
C₃H₈O: same formula does not specify one structure.
1-Propanol: CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–OH
Methoxyethane: CH₃–CH₂–O–CH₃
In 1-propanol, O is attached to C and H. In methoxyethane, O is between two C atoms. Changing those connections gives a different compound: a structural isomer, not a resonance contributor.

Octet Rule: Recognize Its Limits

Learning Objective: Separate duet, electron-deficient, odd-electron, and expanded-shell cases.
CaseExampleElectron accounting
DuetH in H₂O2 surrounding electrons
Electron deficientBF₃B: three bonds, 6 surrounding electrons
Odd electronNO11 total valence electrons; one remains unpaired
Expanded-shell Lewis drawingXeF₄Xe: four bonds + two lone pairs = 12 surrounding electrons
C, N, O, and F are second-period atoms: do not exceed an octet. Some period-3 and heavier central atoms permit expanded-shell Lewis drawings; not every such atom must exceed eight.
Expanded-shell drawings are an electron-counting model. Do not explain them by assuming that empty d orbitals simply accept the extra electrons.
